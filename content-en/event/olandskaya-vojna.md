The Ollandian War is the Dangarian Empire’s invasion of the neighboring Grand Principality of Ollandia. The novel does not present it as a continuous narrative: the reader sees isolated episodes through the eyes of Lieutenant [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort]], Major [[klyajn|Klein]] of the Claws, and civilians affected by the war. Yet these episodes form a picture of the campaign from the fall of its first cities through capitulation and the subsequent preparations for an attack on Catalia. The conquest is the central reason Dietrich finds himself in Ollandia and the Catalian characters fear for the future.

## Background and opposing forces (ch. 4–17)
In chapter four, the narrator takes the reader from Laerta through Ollandia to Dangar. Ollandia is a land of princely palaces, hussars, and orchestras, but not a single locally built locomotive: every steam engine is Catalian. “Haughty” princes divide the country among themselves, competing with one another; their retinues are not united and act without coordination. Prince Wladyslaw Lenz, marshal of the Latsen Voivodeship’s nobility, understood years beforehand that war was inevitable and Ollandia would lose: the empire was enormous and unified, while the Ollandian forces were fragmented. He tried unsuccessfully to unite the retinues and organize joint exercises. At the same time, he prepared for defeat by visiting Hofstadt, cultivating noble eagles, and considering a dynastic marriage between his niece Olkha and a Dangarian family (ch. 17).

The Dangarian army is divided into “eagle” officers and “stone” rank-and-file soldiers, slaves wearing little stones around their necks. Stones are tenacious in line combat but “slow and dull” in mountains and rough terrain, as will become apparent in the north. The empire’s second advantage is the railway: its troops advance along the Plyutva–Latsen line.

## The course of the campaign (ch. 2–31)
**Plyutva and Latsen.** The first major success was the capture of [[plyutva|Plyutva]], which Dangarian artillery almost leveled; later, chapter thirty-one describes it as destroyed to its foundations. During the siege, Dietrich commands a platoon on a captured bastion, but says he has never killed anyone with his own hand. The army then takes [[latsen|Latsen]], the country’s geographical center, splitting it into north and south; Field Marshal Reiniger establishes headquarters in the former nobles’ assembly hall. In autumn—roughly a month after Plyutva’s capture, according to chapter two—the war “moves east,” while Dietrich’s battery remains in the rear to guard a railway halt. This is where the [[tri-zalpa|battle at the well on the hill]] occurs. According to chapter six, Latsen fell three months before this episode; the novel does not explain the discrepancy between the figures.

**The conference in Latsen (ch. 31).** Near Latsen, the troops divide into three armies. A conference with Reiniger at the end of summer supplies the details:
- General of Infantry [[brojenshtoff|Brojenstoff]] (2nd Army) heads north into the mountainous “shepherd” provinces. Dangarian infantry is helpless against Ollandian ambushes and raids: the enemy destroys supply trains and blows up depots while avoiding major engagements. The general dreams of a decisive battle; Reiniger values him mainly as a diversion preventing the northerners from helping the south.
- General of Infantry Kurtzleben (1st Army), a “merciless butcher” with the strongest of the three armies, advances south toward [[bargot|Bargot]], the capital, and the River [[khursla|Khursla]]. Steamships are supposed to supply him by sea and river, but part of the route crosses [[mergana|Mergana]], which lodges a protest and refuses the vessels passage. Diplomats resolve the issue, but by then the steamships have moved to other routes, leaving the cargo to rot in port warehouses. The Kaiser flies into a rage and attacks the fischmeisters (ch. 31). Kurtzleben asks permission to force a crossing without fear of casualties—“I am unfamiliar with the concept of ‘unacceptable losses’”—while the enemy has fortified the southern bank and brought up mortars.
- Lieutenant General Hofmeyer (3rd Army) is, in Klein’s view, the least distinguished but most capable commander. He advances east to take [[mershants|Mershants]] Fortress near Catalia’s borders and complete Ollandia’s division. Two Catalian rifled howitzers defend the fortress, firing four versts, twice as far as Dangarian mortars; Hofmeyer lays a shell from one of these howitzers before the field marshal and says that at least a dozen fall on his positions every day.

**Reiniger’s plan.** Brojenstoff is to keep the northerners pinned down. Kurtzleben receives sixty days to cross the Khursla and besiege Bargot, but must not attack head-on. Four of Hofmeyer’s divisions, halving his army, are to cross upstream and strike the southern Ollandian force’s flank, while he merely maintains the siege of Mershants. Hofmeyer proposes his own plan: in winter, two or three hundred eagles on foot will creep across the frozen marsh to the eastern wall, blow it up, and disable the howitzers. The field marshal rejects the plan as suicidal and orders the divisions to leave within two days under Major General Walbrecht.

**The Claws’ intervention.** Klein, dreaming of a red phoenix patch, sees war as necessary, while the field marshal and generals instead exaggerate the difficulties to dissuade the Kaiser from attacking Catalia. He suggests that Hofmeyer remain in Latsen for fifteen days and return to the front in twenty-five, so that the courier carrying the order “never arrives”: by then, autumn mud will prevent the divisions from marching, and Hofmeyer will have to take Mershants with his entire army. Hofmeyer understands the hint. This scene is examined in detail in [[kogti-kajzera|Kaiser’s Claws]].

## War in the rear and the capital (ch. 6–29)
For Dietrich, war exists in the daily life of occupation rather than on a map. In chapter six, captured Latsen is cleaned up and furnished with an orchestra, but only women stand at the fountain. Land changes hands: occupied parts of Ollandia are being registered anew, enemies’ land is confiscated, heroes are given land, and the registers are “under reform,” so no certificates are issued (ch. 17). The resisting part of the country is considered enemy territory. Nobles in occupied lands face the status “unsicher,” or “unreliable”: their holdings are seized, and between twenty and seventy percent of the population is branded as slaves (ch. 29). Prince Lenz, who surrendered his voivodeship without a fight, hopes to become the Kaiser’s governor in Ollandia and tries to avoid that classification.

In Hofstadt, war is discussed over cards. In chapter fourteen, at the baroness’s fishing event, a millionaire merchant assures Prince Lenz that the war will soon end and that its main aim is to open a way into Catalia: tracks must be laid and trade established. In chapter twenty-five, Dietrich writes to his wife that “everyone says the war will end soon,” that Kurtzleben is advancing decisively, and that he may take the capital by winter.

## Capitulation and preparations against Catalia (ch. 9–34)
The Catalian view of the war appears in several chapters. In chapter nine, Deserey demolishes the battle scenes in Alvaro’s novel and grows gloomy at the word “Solanta.” In chapter twenty-six, the mysterious lady says that in the fourteen years since the Solantan expedition, Catalia has not fought a proper war.

By chapter twenty-eight, six months after Ollandia’s capitulation, Deserey reports that the Dangarian forces have not withdrawn: Kurtzleben’s and [[brojenshtoff|Brojenstoff’s]] armies remain in the center, Hofmeyer’s is at Mershants, the Plyutva–Latsen–Mershants railway is being built, and Hofmeyer now has twenty-five thirty-pounder siege mortars useful only against fortresses. Catalia has fifteen combat-ready divisions; Dangar has three armies of eight divisions each in Ollandia, and a double-track main line would allow another three armies to be moved in—fifty divisions against fifteen. Deserey asks [[fatima|Fatima]] to raise the question of mobilizing two hundred thousand people at the High Table. In chapter thirty-two, Deserey’s diaries reveal his assessment of the Dangarian commanders: Reiniger is a highly experienced coward, Brojenstoff a career soldier, Kurtzleben a butcher, and Hofmeyer the best.

In chapter thirty-four, war unexpectedly returns to Catalia in a different form: according to Alvaro’s theory, Rodrigo da Vira, sold into Dangarian slavery, entered the army and escaped with other stones during the Ollandian War.

## The map and the author’s conception
The map published by the author shows three red arrows from Plyutva to Latsen and onward: north for [[brojenshtoff|Brojenstoff’s]] 2nd Army, south toward Bargot for Kurtzleben’s 1st Army, and southeast toward Mershants for Hofmeyer’s 3rd Army. Dietrich’s battle site is also marked. Mergana lies farther south along the Khursla, while beyond the western edge of the map “Red serves his sentence.” The same map names a town “Ginarva,” while chapter four calls that same railway terminus “Girnava.”

In an interview, the author said that the Dangarian generals are pessimistic about a future war with Catalia, as is Deserey, and that both sides’ rulers fear it; the latter formulation came from the host, and the author did not object.
