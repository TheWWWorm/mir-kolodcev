The destruction of Dunkwald is a mystery connecting two of the novel’s storylines: it prompts the Fishermen’s Guild to interrogate Joriksen and Major Klein to search for a fugitive digger. The catastrophe itself is never shown in any chapter; the reader knows of it only through other characters’ reports.

## Rumors and the newspaper (ch. 14)
Dietrich hears the first reports at Baroness Walbrecht’s fishing event. Over cards, guests discuss the “terrible incident” in Dunkwald: many people have died, including fishermen and officers. Asked whether it is true, Count von Wahrheit, owner of Glory, replies that his newspaper does not print nonsense. In the gazebo, Joriksen tells the baroness quietly that the guild is bewildered, the magnus furious, and Glory has already published the story. Dietrich remembers only a fragment and does not understand what they mean.

## The conversation on the train (ch. 29)
Magnus Amir the Grey himself provides the details in a train compartment, placing a clepsydra on the table to slow time and muffle sound. A postman found Dunkwald full of corpses; no survivors were discovered. Forest animals had scattered the bodies, but had not killed the people: a pack could not overcome four hundred people, including three platoons of soldiers. The town authorities wasted a month, then transferred the case to the provincial capital, where another two months were lost. It all happened in spring, and the magnus’s people reached the site at the end of summer. The bones show tooth and claw marks, knife or bullet wounds, and some bodies were torn to pieces: there were many killers, and not all were human. The records for the year and the final day contain nothing remarkable.

The magnus examines the settlement’s “pits” and reports that not one is a schönbrun. If anyone had dug a schönbrun, he remarks, it would have appeared throughout the records, and Oberfischmeister Landau would not be lying dead but racing to the capital on the fastest train. Landau’s body was not found, but the magnus is certain he died. For the guild, he says, it is a devastating loss: losing another three Dunkwalds would have been easier than losing one oberfischmeister.

The magnus questions Joriksen about dark schönbruns: can monsters escape from wells—according to the science, no; they can only bring illness, bad luck, poverty, despair, and fear—and could Landau have created a dark well? Joriksen answers that Landau was a determined man and would have succeeded if it were possible. The magnus knows Joriksen lied to Flammel, and Joriksen confesses his plan involving the Catalian: he instilled faith in the foreigner, gave him easy conditions, and staged surveillance; the Catalian “simply got better,” while the pit proved an ordinary well. The magnus is surprised that Landau did not take revenge for the deception and remained attached to Dunkwald. In parting, he observes that every pit was examined and none was a schönbrun: the Catalian “didn’t even dig down to water.”

Dietrich, present as an “intermediary,” offers an artilleryman’s explanation: bullets and bayonets could account for the wounds, and canister shot for the torn bodies. He advises looking for shot in the trees and a cannon on the forest road. The magnus thanks him.

## Further steps (ch. 31)
In chapter thirty-one, Joriksen tells Major [[klyajn|Klein]] that Oberfischmeister Flammel and a certain Grey questioned him. He came “close to the truth,” learning only that not a single schönbrun was found in Dunkwald and that “the fellow never reached water.” The Grey has taken an interest in him and will soon take one in Klein. Klein replies that he would still prefer to find the digger, bring him back, and interrogate him.

## What the reader knows from Red’s storyline (ch. 27–33)
Readers following [[red|Red]] know more than the characters. Dunkwald was founded half a century ago to create schönbruns artificially; none of its hundreds of “experimental wells” produced water. Landau, appointed senior master, devised the “beast” Zustand for Red: chained to a post in a pit, he executes condemned prisoners. At the end of chapter thirty-three, army tax collectors, acting on orders from General of Infantry Brauschwitz and citing a recommendation from the Kaiser’s Claws, take Red into the army. Having counted eleven dead soldiers, Landau gives way. As the magnus notes, Landau loved Dunkwald and refused a transfer.

The novel does not say whether the catastrophe occurred that same spring. According to the author, Red’s storyline takes place earlier than Dietrich’s and Alvaro’s, and all three “will fit together in a particular way”; he did not reveal the details.

## What remains unknown
The novel does not reveal what caused the settlement’s destruction, how many killers there were or what they were, Landau’s fate, or Red’s role in these events. The characters’ theories contradict one another: the Grey suspects that the oberfischmeister may have created a dark schönbrun, Joriksen that someone dug a new well, and Dietrich that weapons were involved. No eyewitnesses survived.
