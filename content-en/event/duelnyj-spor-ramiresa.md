The dispute that begins in a restaurant and ends in the Chamber of the Word opens the novel’s plot. It shows how conflicts are settled in [[kataliya|Catalia]] and sets in motion the chain of events that leads Alvaro to the book about the heroes of Solanta and to a new acquaintance with Fatima.

## The quarrel (ch. 1)
Celebrating his book’s success, Alvaro takes his friends Fernan and Lucille Gereira to an expensive restaurant near the Grand Opera. One patron, a solitary gentleman with a pointed beard and expressionless “misty” eyes, politely observes that the establishment is intended for the upper ranks of art and law, yet the maestro has brought two [[obrero|obreros]]. Only now, with acute shame, does Alvaro remember that Fernan and Lucille belong to the Service esfera and are therefore not his equals in rank. His friends read it on his face. He showers the stranger with reproaches, and the man answers with “wisdom”: one equals seven; Alvaro has brought “two sevenths” of a person—as though bringing a dismembered corpse.

Alvaro challenges him to a revolver duel, but the stranger immediately refuses, mockingly apologizing for having “mistaken” Alvaro for a Catalian: the country’s native people are called people of the word. “Our conflicts are settled with words, not bullets,” he says, proposing a dueling debate. Confident of his literary superiority, Alvaro agrees, then notices the bracelet bearing a black snake on his opponent’s wrist: he is a master of law from [[fatima|Allen Fatima’s]] circle. Alvaro offers a stake of one thousand escudos, but his opponent asks for “a month’s income.” They arrange to meet at three the following afternoon.

## The Chamber of the Word (ch. 1)
A debate in the Chamber of the Word requires an arbiter wearing a bracelet of the Law esfera, the duelists, and at least five jurors who know both disputants equally well or neither of them. Jurors receive no money and attend for the spectacle. The clerk records the opponent’s name: Vincente Ramires da Rimini. The arbiter is almost disqualified because he knows Ramires, but he has read Alvaro and values his books, so everyone agrees to his appointment. A little weather vane determines who speaks first, and the right goes to Alvaro.

Alvaro has scarcely prepared: in the morning he read reviews, replied to female readers, ate paella for lunch, and fired two sets of six shots at a target in the courtyard. He briefly and eloquently describes the previous evening’s dinner, believing the case self-evident. But Ramires agrees with every word: he admits behaving unworthily after a reprimand from his superior and insists on just one point. Only a single phrase offended Alvaro—“one equals seven”—and it is true, because people are unequal. The argument becomes a philosophical contest. Alvaro invokes reason, feelings, and descent from the Ancients; Ramires responds with comparisons and examples: a vagrant and the head of an academy; an infant protected from illness but neither asked for advice nor assigned important work. Alvaro then presents his last argument: love. Every person is loved by someone who will mourn them. Ramires produces newspapers reviewing Alvaro’s novels—“half of educated Laerta is in love with the maestro’s work”—and demonstrates that the writer is loved more than everyone else in the hall combined, meaning that by the criterion of love, people are unequal too. Alvaro concedes defeat without waiting for the vote.

The second trap is the amount. Alvaro received seven thousand escudos for the book, but spent fifteen months writing it and tries to divide the sum. Ramires objects: they agreed on “monthly” income, not average monthly income, and monthly income means the income of the current month. The jurors do not object, and the arbiter enters seven thousand escudos in the verdict, payable within three days. Although he admires Alvaro’s writing, the arbiter observes that a duel must never be allowed to descend into truisms; by challenging “one equals seven,” Alvaro had doomed himself.

## Consequences (ch. 3–28)
In chapter three, Alvaro learns at the bank that Ramires’s attorney has already demanded seven thousand escudos, while his account contains only four thousand six hundred: in a month, he has spent a third of his fee on restaurants, theaters, yachts, and women. He covers the remainder with a three-month promissory note. That same evening, Don Filippe offers him a commission: a book about the heroes of Solanta, which will cover the debt and earn him another three thousand escudos. His defeat thus pushes Alvaro into the world of the heroes of Solanta.

In chapter seven, Alvaro is in debt, has dismissed his servants, and lives on rusks; Fernan admits to himself that he is pleased by his friend’s fall. The debate also has later repercussions: when Fatima explains in chapter twenty-eight why she was “trying to figure him out,” she recalls that he “lost seven thousand to my secretary defending an absurd thesis about equality.” This defeat, together with his fighting on the unemployed workers’ side, convinced her that Alvaro was a simpleton rather than Don Filippe’s agent.

The chapter’s title, “One Equals Seven,” quotes the phrase that started everything. The theme of inequality among the esferas returns in Alvaro’s conversations with Fernan and Mor, and in the [[stachka-v-laerte|obrero unrest]].
