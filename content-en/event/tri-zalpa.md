The battle at the well on the hill is the novel’s only battle depicted from a participant’s perspective. It occupies the whole of chapter two and remains the principal cause of everything that happens to Dietrich for more than ten chapters afterward. There is no mystery or magic in the battle except one thing: the lieutenant, an otherwise unremarkable artillery officer, gives orders as if he has seen the outcome in advance, and no one can explain it.

## The eve of battle (ch. 2)
In autumn, roughly a month after the capture of [[plyutva|Plyutva]], the war has “moved east,” while a four-gun battery remains to guard a railway halt with coal depots and water tanks. The guns have women’s names: Mathilda, Berta, Frieda, and Maira. The battery commander is Staff Captain Claus von Zeidvist, a drunkard who despises Lieutenant Dietrich for understanding ballistics better than he does. Speer commands the first platoon, Dietrich the second. A fusilier platoon under NCO Gelb, composed almost entirely of enslaved “stones,” provides cover.

Ordered to inspect the platoon, Dietrich meets two stones. The first is a holy simpleton nicknamed Salvation: he believes the [[seyatel|Sower]] will save everyone good, and keeps talking about “dear water,” the god’s sister. The second is a tall foreigner with “THOU SHALT NOT KILL” branded across his chest, incapable of killing; he is called Killnot. The charcoal burners’ hamlet beside the hill was destroyed before the battery arrived. A well lies in the cellar of one of the ruins, known only to Salvation.

## The night of the escape (ch. 2)
During the night, the platoon’s stones escape after mutilating the NCO: Gelb is found in a hut with his palms and feet nailed down and his jaw smashed. Everyone flees except Salvation, who asks to stay. Dietrich finds this inconceivable because stones have no will. In the morning, horses can be heard snorting in the forest. Salvation persuades the lieutenant to fetch water and leads him to the cellar well. Dietrich looks down; the chapter does not describe what he sees, but as he comes out, he hears bugles.

## Three volleys (ch. 2)
A squadron of Ollandian hussars carrying rifles rides out of the forest, about one hundred and twenty horsemen. Facing them are four unsupported guns, only two ready for action. Furious at the escape, Zeidvist threatens Dietrich with a court-martial and penal servitude; at that moment, Dietrich “sees something.” He whispers to his commander, “You won’t leave me to rot, Herr Staff Captain. I’m afraid you’ll be dead in seven seconds,” and counts aloud. At “one,” a distant stray bullet strikes Zeidvist in the face. By regulation, Speer should take command, but he is pale and obeys Dietrich.

Dietrich then conducts the battle according to a plan he already knows. He sees the stretch of road where the hussars will slow, because the ruins force them to bunch together in a narrow gap between a chapel and a pile of logs.
1. **First volley.** Mathilda and Berta initially fire round shot that ricochets through the racing squadron, then switch to canister; Dietrich orders Frieda and Maira moved to other positions. Canister strikes the hussars at the bottleneck; about thirty men and horses fall.
2. **Second volley.** As the hussars break through and climb the slope, Dietrich orders the muzzles depressed by lifting the gun-carriage trails, then fires at point-blank range. The wave of attackers stops.
3. **Third volley.** A second wave reaches the hilltop and cuts down the crews of Mathilda and Berta, which Dietrich has ordered abandoned; stones who fail to get away die. Frieda and Maira, prepared in advance, fire at the abandoned guns, where the hussars have crowded together.

Everything that seems accidental—a powder cartridge dropped in a puddle, the delay of two guns, stones lingering too long—appears, from the text, to be known to Dietrich beforehand. The battery loses seventeen men; not one of the dead was free.

## After the battle (ch. 2)
Descending the hill, Dietrich experiences a strange, burning sense of triumph. He finds Salvation by the well, rejoicing: “Dear water saved us all!” The simpleton wants to tell everyone. Dietrich wraps his pistol in his cloak and shoots him in the back of the head. The text does not state why.

## Investigation and consequences (ch. 6–31)
A day or two later in Latsen, Major [[klyajn|Wolfgang von Klein]] of the Claws summons the lieutenant to certify his recommendation for promotion, but instead retells the battle as a crime: Dietrich supposedly ordered the stones to kill the NCO and flee, while his batman [[milosh|Milosh]] shot Zeidvist during a volley. Dietrich defends his innocence by pointing out that he had no weapon with him, and that Salvation and Gelb are already dead. Klein suspends him “until the investigation is complete” and sends him to [[khofshtadt|Hofstadt]].

The well remains Dietrich’s secret. In chapter fourteen, he tells master fisherman Joriksen that he found a [[kolodets|schönbrun]] in Ollandia: a village once used the well, but the village is gone and the stone who found it is dead. During his “spontaneous fishing,” he saw a faceless man with a mouth in his belly fighting a pack of small beasts. In chapter twenty-one, Dietrich learns from the guild’s atlas that the land by the railway halt belongs to Prince Wladyslaw Lenz. In chapter twenty-nine, Count von Lurd threatens the prince: on his land, a hussar squadron attacked the station and was repelled by a battery; Lenz insists that Cavalry Captain Lipshits acted without his knowledge. In chapter thirty-one, Klein finds the well himself and shows it to Joriksen: a decomposing corpse lies below, and the major is certain Dietrich shot the man in the back of the head.
