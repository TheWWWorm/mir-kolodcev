Claus von Zeidvist is the staff captain commanding the battery to which Lieutenant [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort]] belongs. Chapter 2 opens with Zeidvist's letter to Colonel Walter Gerlach assessing Dietrich; soon afterward, the staff captain dies, and his death remains one of the acts that torments Dietrich most.

## Character (ch. 2)

Zeidvist is abrupt and irritable. Dietrich suspects the battery may have been left in the rear because of its commander's fondness for schnapps. In his letter, Zeidvist describes the twenty-three-year-old lieutenant as unprepossessing, soft, disinclined to duel, too lenient with his orderly [[milosh|Milosh]], and given to saying “umm” and “yes?..”; he has no inclination to promote Dietrich.

Zeidvist is rude to his subordinates. Through his adjutant, he orders Dietrich to inspect the fusilier platoon and bring water—the staff captain wants to wash—and when the lieutenant returns empty-handed, he unleashes a tirade and orders him to bring ten buckets personally the next morning.

## Death (ch. 2–6)

When an Ollandian squadron emerges from the forest at dawn, an enraged Zeidvist threatens Dietrich with a court-martial and penal servitude. Dietrich whispers to him, “I am afraid you will die in seven seconds.” He counts aloud, and at “one,” a stray bullet from an impossibly long distance strikes the staff captain in the face. Dietrich then assumes command of the battery, and the first platoon's commander, [[shpeer|Speer]], obeys him. Later, the battery adjutant will write in his report that just before dying, the staff captain raised his sword at Dietrich, who moved his lips as though praying, and then the bullet struck (ch. 6).

## After His Death (ch. 6–14)

In ch. 6, in [[latsen|Latsen]], Major [[klyajn|Klein]] reads out the reports and constructs a theory in which Dietrich knew the time of the staff captain's death: he ordered his orderly to shoot him under cover of a volley and spent the entire night measuring the slope and counting seconds. Dietrich privately knows he could have pushed Zeidvist out of the bullet's path and did not; he names this as one of the three acts of his own free will that torment him. In ch. 10, he tries to tell Mari about the battle in which “von Zeidvist died,” but breaks off. In ch. 14, when Mari learns that the money was spent to gain her love, his wife's face undergoes the same changes as Zeidvist's in the seconds before the shot.

## At the End of the Novel (ch. 2)

Zeidvist dies in ch. 2 and does not appear again. His unfavorable assessment of Dietrich resurfaces during Klein's interrogation (ch. 6).
