Olkha Lenz is an Ollandian princess, niece and ward of [[lents|Wladyslaw Lenz]], prince of Latsen. She meets Dietrich von Dort in summer in occupied Latsen, then encounters him again in Dangar’s capital, where her uncle is seeking a governorship. In the novel, Olkha belongs to the “Dangarian” storyline: through her, Dietrich enters the capital’s noble circles and the engagement plot that saves him from the Kaiser’s Claws.

## Character and appearance
Olkha has curly light-brown hair, clear blue eyes, and a mole. Dietrich notices her slender neck and its mole, her “amusing curls,” and her lively eyes. She is comically clumsy: at their first meeting she overturns a cup and burns herself, then accidentally dips her cuff into the tea. At boarding school, when she met Katrina, she was looking for a lost earring that had been hanging from her sleeve all along. She acknowledges this habit of small mishaps herself: “Ow! There I go again…”

She jokes with a perfectly straight face, leaving others unsure whether she is serious. She mixes up words, is absentminded, fears men, and tries to blend into the curtains in company. Yet when needed she is decisive and can improvise. Olkha dislikes the Dangarian nobility and does not hide it: the ritual of “feeding” stone servants strikes her as slavery and hypocrisy. Ollandia knew no slavery before the Dangarian troops arrived, and this matters to her.

At boarding school, Olkha learned Dangarian, natural sciences, court etiquette, literature, and poetry. She was not taught to flirt: her uncle demanded that she “not think about foolishness,” and she obeyed.

## Meeting in Latsen (ch. 6)
In summer, three months after Dangarian troops capture Latsen, Dietrich sits in a restaurant on the central square. A young Ollandian woman at the next table burns herself with tea, and he invites her to join him. At first she pretends not to know Dangarian, then speaks it quite fluently. She calls Dietrich “Mr. Occupier,” introduces herself as Olkha — “Helga in your language” — and asks whether he is ashamed to kill. Dietrich invokes his duty of honor. She says she lives outside the town near Mitnaya Oakwood and believes in a simple remedy for trouble: want something badly enough, and a miracle will happen. Dietrich cautiously asks whether there is a “Beautiful Well” in their district; she replies that there is not, and she would know. He retells the parable of the [[seyatel|Sower]]. Then his orderly [[milosh|Milosh]] runs up: Dietrich is summoned to Major [[klyajn|von Klein]]. The encounter leads no further: he does nothing to arrange another meeting.

## Fishing at the baroness’s (ch. 10, 14)
Dietrich learns of the fishing at Baroness Walbrecht’s schönbrun from a newspaper in chapter 10 and secretly spends two hundred marks of the family’s money on a ticket. At the reception ([[rybalka-valbrekht|Fishing at Baroness Walbrecht’s]]), [[elza|Elsa]] introduces Prince Wladyslaw Lenz, the “illustrious descendant of the oldest Ollandian family,” and his niece to the guests. Dietrich recognizes the girl from Latsen. She looks at him with fear, shame, or a plea for help.

The presence of an Ollandian prince in Hofstadt while war continues in the east provokes an insulting remark from a dragoon cornet. Dietrich smooths over the scandal with a toast “to worthy former adversaries who will become allies.” The same cornet later forcibly takes Olkha away from her waltz partner, and she submits in fright. During the “feeding” of servants, the nobles stand behind the stones and place the last spoonful in their mouths as ritual requires. Olkha whispers her outrage at the custom. Dietrich explains that the Sower commanded people to care for one another. She manages to ask him to come on Saturday: she needs to go somewhere, and her uncle will not let her go alone. Meanwhile, the prince asks the baroness to take his niece under her protection.

## Katrina’s cousin (ch. 17)
Saturday. Dietrich arrives at the Astoria Hotel. Olkha runs out wearing one shoe and explains that the prince is moving to the baroness’s house, while she is going to visit her friend Katrina. She needs an escort because her uncle will not let her visit alone. When the lieutenant defended the prince and his niece at the baroness’s reception, Olkha presented him to her uncle as Dietrich von Eyring of a cadet branch of the [[ejring|Eyring]] family, Katrina’s cousin. Thus artillery lieutenant von Dort becomes an “Eyring.”

On the way, she explains the background: her uncle’s plans, Saint Adelia’s boarding school, the graduation ball in Schlissel, and [[katrina|Katrina’s]] marriage to Count [[lurd|Ludwig von Lurd]]. The evening goes well: Katrina readily plays along with the story, and she and Dietrich invent a shared childhood. On the way back in the carriage, Dietrich kisses Olkha. She protests weakly, then they kiss again, and Olkha asks him to stop. Dietrich later remembers this evening as a closeness of souls rather than a romance; he has a wife, [[mari|Mari]].

## Silence and the night at the mansion (ch. 21–25)
After the kiss, Dietrich initially thinks of her as a means of gaining access to the prince’s land: in the Fishermen’s Guild atlas, he sees that the plot containing the dark schönbrun near the station belongs to Lenz, and hopes Olkha will help. He writes to her twice, but there is no reply. (Milosh swears that he gave both letters to the butler.)

In chapter 25, Dietrich comes uninvited to the baroness’s mansion. Olkha greets him coldly, with anger and shame. He follows her into the guest wing and knocks; she begs him to leave, but he enters. Olkha stands in a dressing gown with nothing underneath; clothes are scattered across the floor, and Katrina sits on the bed in her underwear. Katrina tears Dietrich’s shirt, scratches him, throws her belongings out of the window, and jumps after them. Olkha unlocks the door for the prince. Lenz demands pistols and calls Katrina a procuress. Olkha falls to her knees and pleads for Dietrich, but her uncle drives her away.

The novel does not explicitly say what was happening in the room before Dietrich arrived. Later, Dietrich himself blames Katrina for everything, while Count Lurd says that Olkha was expecting a suitor. The narrator calls Katrina an “adulteress” with a secret the lieutenant could expose.

## Engagement (ch. 29)
Count Lurd proposes a wedding instead of a duel: the prince would gain a connection to an ancient Dangarian family. Olkha and Dietrich remain silent in fear: anyone could reveal his real surname. The prince agrees and demands a written proposal from Dietrich, who asks him for a hundred-desyatina plot of land. In the morning, at the station, Magnus [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]] speaks of Olkha’s “bad reputation”; Dietrich defends her passionately, only to discover that it was a test.

## Dietrich’s fiancée (ch. 31)
While Dietrich is held at the gendarmerie in Latsen, Olkha and Katrina burst into the office and demand her fiancé’s release. The gendarmes have found an engagement agreement on him, with the plot near the station as dowry. Katrina secures his release by invoking her husband and Margrave von Eyring, while Olkha pushes Major [[klyajn|Klein]] toward the door, shouting “Into the cell, jailer!” and throws her arms around Dietrich’s neck. She knows nothing of the [[kolodets|schönbrun]] near the station, and Dietrich pretends not to understand the major’s words. This is the last information the novel gives about her.

## Relationships
- [[lents|Wladyslaw Lenz]] — her uncle and guardian. Calls her an “idiot” for failing to cultivate connections at boarding school.
- [[katrina|Katrina von Lurd]] — her best friend since boarding school.
- [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort]] — “Mr. Occupier,” then the supposed “Eyring,” then her fiancé.
- [[elza|Elsa von Walbrecht]] — her patron and mistress of the house where Olkha stays in Hofstadt.
