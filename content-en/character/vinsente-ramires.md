**Vincente Ramires da Rimini** is a master of law, a solitary man with a pointed beard and sharp mustache. In the first chapter, he sits in an expensive restaurant near the Grand Opera, notices [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] and his friends at the next table, and remarks that the establishment is for the higher ranks of Art and Law, yet the maestro has brought two [[obrero|obreros]]. This remark sparks the dueling debate described in detail in [[duelnyj-spor-ramiresa|“The Dueling Debate with Ramires”]].

## The restaurant (ch. 1)
Ramires responds to an insult with “wisdom”: one equals seven; by bringing two obreros, Alvaro has brought “two-sevenths of a person,” as though a dismembered corpse. Alvaro challenges him to a revolver duel, but Ramires refuses: native Catalians are people of the word and settle conflicts with words. He proposes a [[duelnyj-spor|dueling debate]]. Alvaro offers a stake of a thousand escudos, while Ramires asks for “a month’s income.” He wears a bracelet with a black snake, from which Alvaro deduces that his opponent serves [[fatima|Fatima the allen]].

## The debate in the Chamber of the Word (ch. 1)
The following day in the [[palata-slova|Chamber of the Word]], Ramires unexpectedly agrees with everything his opponent says: he admits that he was angry the day before after a reprimand from his superiors and behaved unworthily, but insists that Alvaro was offended only by the phrase “one equals seven,” which is true because people are not equal. Alvaro loses his bearings and appeals to reason, emotions, and descent from the [[drevnie|Ancients]]; Ramires counters with ship analogies, questions about criteria, and examples. In desperation, Alvaro says that everyone is loved by someone. Ramires then produces newspapers containing reviews of Alvaro’s novels and demonstrates that the writer is loved more than everyone present, so people are unequal in love too. Asked whether anyone loves him, he answers that only his cat does.

The second trap concerns the amount. Alvaro received seven thousand escudos for his book but spent fifteen months writing it; Ramires secures a ruling that “a month’s income” means the income received in the current month. The arbiter includes this in his verdict. He also instructs Alvaro that one must not let a debate descend into platitudes. Later, in ch. 3, Ramires sends an attorney demanding seven thousand, and Alvaro is forced to issue a promissory note.

## Debating style
Ramires structures his argument by catching his opponent out when he resorts to platitudes, quoting reviews of his opponent’s work, and leading him to the required conclusion. In the text, he speaks evenly, without anger, and uses his own concessions on minor points as a technique.

## His place among Fatima’s lawyers (ch. 20–28)
In ch. 20, Fernan remarks that the lawyer who faced Alvaro was far from the best of Fatima’s “little serpents.” In ch. 28, [[fatima|Fatima]] herself calls Ramires her secretary: Alvaro lost “seven thousand to my secretary, defending an absurd proposition about equality.” She says she made inquiries about Alvaro in advance, and the debate was part of the information she gathered.
