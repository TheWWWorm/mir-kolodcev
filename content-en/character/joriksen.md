**Fritjof Joriksen** is a Dangarian [[fishmajster|fischmeister]], a master fisherman known to the reader through two of the novel's storylines. In [[red|Red's]] storyline, he is the young head of the Dangarian camp at Dunkwald, where slaves are forced to dig wells in the hope of producing a [[kolodets|schönbrun]]; in [[ditrikh|Dietrich's]] storyline, he is already a first-rank master, accepting expensive commissions from the owners of Wells. In an interview, the author named him as one of the Dangarians he likes.

## Character and Appearance
Joriksen has sharp cheekbones, a hawkish nose, and green eyes; in Dunkwald, he wears a purple cloak with a silver catfish-shaped brooch and a black square cap, while in the capital he wears a white cloak and a gold-trimmed tricorne, with a foreign naval uniform full of pockets beneath the cloak. The master is energetic, proud, and irritable when his plans go awry. His favorite sayings are “money is dust” and “a schönbrun is no shop, and I am no merchant.” He speaks like a trader, yet sincerely believes in the subject of his craft and in the people on whom he stakes his hopes. He swears “by clear water.”


## The Camp in Dunkwald (ch. 4–19)
In Chapter 4, Joriksen receives the newly arrived Red in a hot office with a clock whose pendulum is shaped like a fish. He has learned Catalian words from a dictionary in advance and honestly announces that Red will receive freedom, money, clothes, and a train ticket once he digs a “schönbrun.” He refuses the request to send a letter, but after testing Red with a coin, decides he has found “luck.”

He seldom appears among the slaves, but keeps track of the digging. He uses a dictionary to announce Red's sentence for damaging Guild property—the beaten foreman counts as its property: twenty lashes (ch. 12). After Red's escape, he asks [[yakka|Yakka]] to bring the fugitive back and supports him before Baron [[landau|Landau]] (ch. 15): asked “who are you?”, Red finally answers “I am a Catalian,” and the master is satisfied. He permits Red's letter to be sent with [[greta|Greta]], prescribes a “new Zustand”—digging at night with Catalian songs—and writes “Sorge um dich” (“I care for you”) on the envelope. His favorite phrase during this period is “I to hope in you.”

Red finds this method strange, but in Chapter 15 he understands: the fire, the songs, the night, a foreigner's loneliness, the absence of a brand, and the beautiful Greta are a “recipe” for a schönbrun tailored to his nature. Klein later confirms that the whole scheme was built on inspiring the foreigner (ch. 27). In Chapter 19, Joriksen keeps a record book—the depth of the pit, the number of songs and coughing fits—and visits the clearing himself every morning.

## The Truth About the Plan (ch. 23–27)
Klein's account to Red in ch. 27 reveals how everything was arranged. Joriksen and Wolf—then still a young fischmeister and lieutenant—ended up in Dunkwald because that is where the unlucky and low-born are sent. They became friends on the journey. Joriksen devised a plan: find a foreigner who did not know a schönbrun could not be dug and inspire him, because Dangarians dig out of fear, and the Sower abhors cowards. Red proved to be the right man. When Landau was sent to the camp, Joriksen feared that the glory would go to the baron and “inspired” Red a second time by sending his letter and giving him songs.

He noticed that Red's cough was easing and worked out the truth: the Catalian had never had sarcoma—the Laertan doctor had mistaken bronchitis for it, and the cold and damp earth had made it worse. Through Wolf, Joriksen arranged a forged letter about his mother's illness and submitted a transfer request; the baron put it off. When Red reached water and the foreman, secretly acting in concert with Landau, tried to bury him, the baron examined the sick man, decided the schönbrun had cured him, and immediately signed the request. Joriksen left, leaving Red a letter in broken Catalian; in Chapter 23, he is said to have gone “to his sick mother.” According to Klein, the baron cannot take revenge on Joriksen: a complaint would mean admitting he had been fooled. He will take it all out on Red.

When Red clears away the debris again and shows the water, Landau sees clear water without mist or monsters: the well proves to be an ordinary one, not a schönbrun. Klein explains this to Red with the word “Täuschung”—deception. By then, Joriksen is no longer in Dunkwald.

## Hofstadt: Dietrich and Elsa (ch. 14–21)
In Chapter 14, Joriksen conducts a “fishing session” in Baroness [[elza|Elsa von Walbrecht's]] Beautiful Well with his assistant [[viglaf|Wiglaf]]. He requires every guest to write down the heartache they wish to be rid of and checks everything by the sea reckoning, down to the number of people: there are thirty-six guests instead of thirty-five, and the master chooses poor Dietrich as the “extra” one (see [[rybalka-valbrekht|Fishing at Baroness Walbrecht's]] for details). Dietrich later tells him of the schönbrun he has found, and Joriksen does not believe him: none has been discovered for half a century.

In Chapter 21, the master receives Dietrich at his mansion, which is arranged “inside out”: an unprepossessing wing faces the street, while the main house is hidden behind trees. Inside is a museum of curiosities: fish skeletons, a rusty cannon covered in coral, a collection of money from around the world, and a Muari icon given to him by an emir. The master says every schönbrun has its own temperament and there are no formulas: intuition and experience are required. His services cost five hundred marks a day; studying a new schönbrun costs at least ten thousand, which he is willing to “forgive” in exchange for a year's exclusive fishing rights. The mortgage deed lists Dietrich's son [[khajnts|Heinz]] as collateral. He knows a great deal about the lieutenant, including that the Claws are investigating him, and fears Dietrich may let something slip. After Dietrich presents his own conditions, he agrees to pay him three hundred marks a month “for silence” and swear “by clear water.”

## Dunkwald, the Magnus, and Klein (ch. 29–31)
On learning of the events in Dunkwald, Joriksen sends word through Milosh and Otto demanding that Dietrich leave for Ollandia that very day. Waiting in the train compartment is Magnus [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]], Joriksen's teacher, who tells him of Dunkwald's destruction: a postman found around four hundred dead. The Grey knows Joriksen lied to Oberfischmeister [[flammel|Flammel]], and Joriksen admits to the plan involving the Catalian. The Grey does not understand why Landau took no revenge; Joriksen and Otto swear they know nothing.

In Chapter 31, it emerges that both Flammel and the Grey himself interrogated Joriksen, that he “passed close to the truth,” and that the master remains in danger. He presented Dietrich to the Grey as an intermediary between himself and a client, and this was believed. Klein has arrested the lieutenant, found the well, and now demands that Joriksen study it within two weeks for General Hofmeyer's army; in return, he promises him a chance to become an oberfischmeister. Joriksen objects that one cannot rush a schönbrun and that Otto, too, is in danger with him, but obeys.

## Relationships
- [[klyajn|Wolfgang von Klein]]—an old friend; they met on the road to Dunkwald. Otto, Joriksen's secretary, is Klein's son.
- [[red|Red]]—“his” Catalian; in Chapter 31, Joriksen still remembers the phrase “You will be able to dig a schönbrun.”
- [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]]—magnus and teacher.
- [[landau|Landau]]—his superior in Dunkwald, whose revenge Joriksen escaped.
- [[ditrikh|Dietrich]]—a debtor and a valuable, dangerous witness.
- [[elza|Elsa von Walbrecht]]—a client and, judging by the tone of their conversations, someone close to him.

## Conception and Interviews
According to the author, he likes Master Joriksen, whose name appears as “Yurikson” in the interview transcript. The interviewer noted that the master appears in both Dietrich's and Red's storylines and has had time to be promoted; the author confirmed that the storylines are offset in time. The interviewer also suggested that Joriksen belongs to a conquered “Scandinavian” people related to the Dangarians; the author did not contradict this. In the book itself, Joriksen is described as a Chinkhar with some northern blood (ch. 14, 27).

## At the End of the Novel (ch. 33)
In Chapter 33, Joriksen appears only in conversation: in the capital, Baron Landau speaks warmly of him to the magnus. The master's fate after Chapter 31 is not shown in the novel.
