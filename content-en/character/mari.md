Mari von Dort is [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort’s]] wife, living with their little son [[khajnts|Heinz]] in a rented apartment on Witwenplatz in [[khofshtadt|Hofstadt]]. She appears only in Dietrich’s scenes, always in connection with money and domestic peace. Unwittingly, she sets a chain of events in motion: to regain her love, Dietrich spends almost all their savings on a fishing party hosted by Baroness [[elza|Elsa von Walbrecht]], then, left without funds, agrees to a deal with the fischmeister [[joriksen|Joriksen]].

## Character and appearance

Mari is frugal and practical to the point of pedantry. On Sundays she puts exactly the amount that may be spent that week into her purse, adding not a pfennig until the following Sunday; the rest stays in a hiding place behind the mirror. She runs the household firmly, repeatedly scolding and correcting the stone maid, and expects her husband to respect her rules at home. Dietrich regards her character with respect and fear. He believes there is more will and life in his wife’s little finger than in all of Princess [[olkha|Olkha]], which is precisely why her weakness, when it appears on one occasion, throws him off balance.

As to her appearance, the book mentions thick hair; after their long separation, she feels unusually soft to her husband’s touch. By birth she is an eagle woman from a proud but very poor family, as recorded in the Kaiser’s Claws’ dossier.

## Dietrich’s return (ch. 10)

When Dietrich enters the house after a month’s journey, Mari is bathing Heinz and scolding the maid. Water costs as much as wine during the month of Gehai, and before her husband can embrace her, she sends him to the bathhouse: he is dirty and, she says, surely has lice. Dietrich’s letter from [[latsen|Latsen]] announcing his discharge never reached her, so she expects a man on leave and receives an officer under investigation and without pay. At first she suspects infidelity, then becomes furious over money: there are no savings, there will be no salary, and they must live. She goes to the child to cry and refuses to listen to excuses or to attempts to explain that his conscience tormented him.

The next day she relents, and they spend it together: the pram, a coffeehouse, stories about Heinz’s first steps and events in the city. She questions him jealously about Ollandian women, but jokingly. When Dietrich starts looking for employment, their relationship improves: Mari greets him dressed up, with the table laid. Dietrich, however, begins to suspect that her tenderness is “a carrot for a donkey” and depends on the prospect of pay. After a quarrel in which he lets slip something about “a stain on his soul,” Mari concludes that he has a mistress and stops listening. When he brings home his drunken orderly [[milosh|Milosh]] at night and tries to put him to bed in the house, she grabs their son and threatens to leave. Dietrich has to yield and swear never to do it again.

## The missing money (ch. 14)

Returning from Walbrecht’s fishing party, Dietrich finds Mari on the floor beneath a crack in the wall: the hiding place is empty. She has already told Frau [[gizeke|Gieseke]] everything, and the landlady has had the stones searched. Mari asks her husband to question Milosh: if he is the thief, she will forgive him so long as he returns the money. Dietrich confesses that he took the money. Mari is initially glad that the money is safe, then counts what remains and discovers that two hundred marks have been spent on a ticket. Hearing that the wish he wrote on his slip was “for Mari to love me as much as I love her,” she at first cannot believe he is serious, then recoils against the wall and says he has killed them. But the matter does not end in a quarrel: she cries against his chest, repeating that she already loves him and that “this wasn’t worth going fishing for.” Shaken, Dietrich promises to recover the money; Mari replies that he will not find it, but she will think of something.

## Estrangement and a “secret affair” (ch. 17–25)

In ch. 17, Dietrich returns late from Katrina and Count [[lurd|von Lurd]], smelling of wine and perfume. Mari has locked the door and sent word that he is not to come in; Milosh invents a story about the “governor-general” for her and Frau Gieseke, while his master sleeps on a couch in the entrance hall. Later, Dietrich leaves a letter and a hundred and fifty marks under the door, passing the money off as an advance from his employment, and moves into hotel rooms. His reason is the guilt weighing on him for signing the pledge naming their son as collateral (see the article [[khajnts|on Heinz]] for details); at home, he could not look his wife in the eye.

In ch. 25, Mari comes to the hotel herself, asks forgiveness for her jealousy, and asks whether he loves her. Dietrich tells her that the fischmeister merely introduced him to the deputy minister, withholding the truth about Joriksen, Olkha, and the pledge. For several days, Mari leaves their son with the maid and comes running to him: she enjoys this “secret affair” with her own husband, blossoms, and asks for intimacy every day. Dietrich, meanwhile, thinks increasingly of Olkha.

## Parting (ch. 29)

Learning that Dietrich must urgently leave for the front because “the company has suffered losses,” Mari weeps and reminds him that he promised to serve at headquarters. He promises to write and tell her when the company is ready for battle. Asked whether she would agree, as a last resort, to leave for [[muaris|Muaris]], she is initially frightened by the word “disgrace,” then immediately changes her mind: they discharge him when rewards are handed out and call him back when someone must die for the Kaiser. She is ready to travel with Heinz at his first word. In this scene, Mari explicitly sides with her husband against authority for the first time.

Major [[klyajn|Klein]] knows that Mari is Dietrich’s wife, not his fiancée (ch. 31); when the engagement agreement with Olkha is found on Dietrich during his arrest, he is astonished that the married lieutenant has managed matters so cleverly. Mari herself knows nothing of the document, and the novel does not say whether she learns of it later.

## Relationships

- **[[ditrikh|Dietrich]]** loves her and fears losing her, but tells her the truth about neither [[zejdvist|Zeidvist’s]] death, the Well, nor the pledge. He himself observes that there is no intimacy between them until money appears.
- **[[khajnts|Heinz]]** — the son she raises almost alone: Dietrich went to war when the boy was two months old.
- **[[gizeke|Frau Gieseke]]** — the landlady; considers Mari a soon-to-be widow and helps her with water and cloth for diapers.
- **[[milosh|Milosh]]** — her husband’s orderly; Mari will not let him across the threshold when dirty and does not want him sleeping in the house, but suspects him first when the money vanishes and is ready to forgive the theft if only the money is returned.

## At the end of the novel (ch. 29)

Mari is last seen in ch. 29: she sees her husband off on the train to Ollandia and asks him to write every day. Neither his letters nor her replies appear in the novel.
