Alvaro Flores da Silva is a writer from [[laerta|Laerta]], one of the three main characters of Traces on the Water and the only one who introduces himself to the reader: he writes the preface in the first person before switching to the third. He earns his living from novels, gives lectures to aspiring writers, and dreams of a gold bracelet and a place beside his grandfather on the [[svitok|Great Scroll]]. His storyline is an investigation: to write the commissioned book about the heroes of the [[solantijskaya-ekspeditsiya|Solantan Expedition]], he begins looking into the strange death of one of them and gradually finds himself at the center of events far more dangerous than literary arguments.

## Origins and family (ch. 0–13)
Flores is the name of a family “rich in poets, writers, and playwrights.” Of the entire family, only Alvaro’s grandfather, [[rikardo-flores|Ricardo Flores]], produced a masterpiece: his novel The Snake in the Reeds brought its author fame and wealth, and his name was inscribed on the Scroll. Alvaro’s father, Ricardo’s son, “was lost in his father’s radiance” and spent his life as an obscure playwright at a minor theater; he received his maestro’s bracelet late in life, a year before alcoholism ruined him. No inheritance remained: in Alvaro’s own words, all he received from his father was knowledge and debts. The novel does not separately explain the second part of his name, da Silva.

Alvaro sees his own career as a continuation of his grandfather’s: he is determined to become the second member of the family whose name appears on the Scroll. In his youth, as he jokes himself, he was destined to become a womanizing poet, but he “despises predestination” and became a prose writer instead.

## Character and appearance
Alvaro is handsome and knows it: brown eyes, sensual lips, and shoulder-length black hair; he favors a gray hat with a black ribbon and expensive suits. He is ambitious, vain, and quick to fall in love, yet also naive, and readily describes himself as bold and full of vigor. On his left arm he wears the silver [[braslet|bracelet]] of a maestro of art, and at his belt a six-shot revolver. He enjoys shooting as a pastime and practices in the backyard, but at the beginning of the novel he has not shed a drop of blood, and the only duel of his life ended in mutual apologies.

The narrator emphasizes his changeability. Alvaro’s artistic moods shift abruptly: inspiration gives way to certainty of his own lack of talent, and a single letter is enough to destroy his desire to write. He describes this “inner enemy” to his students as an example of a character’s flaw, and no one guesses that it is a self-portrait. He is extravagant: he spends money on restaurants, theaters, yachts, and women, takes advances from the dean for lectures, and disappears for weeks. His relationships with women have followed a pattern: [[malena|Malena]] is too jealous, while his former lover Elma talked too much, and both, in his view, “get in the way of creating.” He calls the mirna of art his only constant beloved.

In his profession, he is certain of only one thing: that he is good. His lectures are lively and mocking: he insists that a protagonist needs poverty and hardship (“a hungry wolf, not a well-fed tomcat”), believes that a novel has no “message,” only a panorama of life, and is convinced that literature should raise people toward honor and courage. He is proud that Catalians are “people of the word,” and several times in the novel it is this pride that prevents him from acting like a coward.

Alvaro does not share his friends’ political views: he believes Catalia is wisely organized and that people become dons through ability. Yet he has long ceased to notice that his friends are not his equals in rank, and his very first confrontation with this fact throws him off balance (see below).

## The dueling debate with Ramires (ch. 0–3)
One evening on Del Toro Boulevard, Alvaro is walking with his friends, the copyist [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan Gereira]] and his wife [[lyusil|Lucille]]. They discuss reforms to the High Table, while Alvaro would rather talk about his new book and the opera Señorita Violet. To treat his friends, he takes them to an expensive restaurant near the Grand Opera. There, a stranger with a pointed beard declares that the establishment is not for [[obrero|obreros]], yet the maestro has brought two of them. Alvaro instantly remembers that his friends are not his equals in rank, and his shame turns to rage. The stranger replies with a piece of “wisdom”: “one equals seven.” Alvaro challenges him to a revolver duel, but his opponent proposes a [[duelnyj-spor|dueling debate]]. He is the master of law [[vinsente-ramires|Vincente Ramires da Rimini]], a member of [[fatima|Fatima’s]] circle; the stake is a month’s income.

Alvaro goes to the [[palata-slova|Chamber of the Word]] the next day unprepared and certain of victory. Ramires first agrees with everything he says, then turns the argument toward inequality between people and demonstrates that the writer himself is loved more than all the jurors put together, meaning that people are unequal in love as well. The second trap is the amount: the fee for his fourth novel, seven thousand escudos, is accepted as his monthly income, even though he spent fifteen months writing it. For details, see [[duelnyj-spor-ramiresa|“The Dueling Debate with Ramires”]]. Alvaro must pay within three days; he has only 4,600 escudos in his bank account, uses the money remaining to him, and issues a promissory note for the shortfall.

Afterward, he dismisses his servants, lives on rusks, and expects no new fee for at least six months; all that remains of his social standing is his bracelet and his house on Foucault Street in the white district, where there is not even a lock.

## Filippe’s commission (ch. 3)
Alvaro remains a lecturer at the [[akademiya-iskusstv|Academy of Arts]]: in class, he identifies the qualities a protagonist should have and unexpectedly adds poverty to the list, unaware that he himself will soon illustrate it. After his visit to the bank, he is invited to a box at the [[grand-opera|Grand Opera]] in the name of [[filippe|Don Filippe di Berro]], prim-allen of Art. After Señorita Violet, Filippe commissions him to write a book about the heroes of the Solantan Expedition. These are four friends of Filippe whose names are on the Scroll, plus a fifth, Doctor [[khuanes|Juanes]], who died the day before their conversation and is added to the list in black eyebrow pencil. His task is to interview the heroes and describe their lives sixteen years later. Filippe promises to cover the entire debt and pay more for the outline and the completed manuscript. Alvaro personally knows and respects two people on the list, the [[karlos|pirate]] and the [[dezerej|general]]; he remarks that the manufacturer [[kristobal-maduro|Maduro]] makes excellent revolvers, while the fourth person on the list, a woman with an exalted title, [[fatima|Fatima]], already intimidates him.

Fernan and Lucille, who have always been short of money themselves, decide to help their ruined friend: Fernan becomes his secretary, with Alvaro setting the condition that he must not use anything he learns for political purposes.

## Juanes’s death and the first heroes (ch. 5–9)
Alvaro and Fernan first visit the late Juanes’s house. The butler, [[emil|Emil Diale]], and the widow tell them about the doctor’s last day. The only thing that catches the writer’s attention is an anonymous letter made from newspaper cuttings (“I know everything. You will not escape retribution”), which he secretly takes. Alvaro immediately decides that Juanes was murdered, although it happened in a white district, where violence is forbidden; Fernan reminds him of this. Later, he uses the same visit as material for a lecture on plot openings (“a corpse is wonderful”). For more, see [[smert-khuanesa|“The Death of Doctor Juanes”]].

In chapter 7, the friends visit Don Carlos el Niña at his old fort. He greets the “scribblers” with a joke involving a double-barreled pistol (an operatic prank that makes Fernan faint), turns out to be a smuggler rather than a corsair, and Alvaro immediately invents a heroic episode for him. Carlos’s wife, Doña [[izabel|Isabel]], offers to show the guests the Well; in the fort’s cellar, it fills Alvaro and the others with bitter melancholy. The next morning, at Deserey’s invitation, Alvaro and Fernan travel to [[ortiyar|Ortiyar]] with [[beatris|Beatriz]], his student nicknamed Golden Quill, who arrives in a steam-powered Esperanza. The general analyzes the battle scenes in Alvaro’s novel, and the offended writer vows revenge on Beatriz. On the return journey, he imagines that they are being followed: a green car trails the friends, but the driver will turn out to be the divorced chauffeur Ugo Martinez.

## The demonstration, the finger, and Maduro (ch. 11–13)
Beatriz races Alvaro back to Laerta and offers to take Deserey’s diaries for safekeeping. To rid himself of his persistent student, he instead writes to her father, Judge [[sudya-enkhal|Enhal]], citing the surveillance he does not himself believe in. This lie will later cost him Beatriz’s trust. Returning to Laerta, they encounter a protest by dismissed workers: [[obrero|obreros]] stop a train carrying steam-powered machines for Don [[balboa|Balboa]] ([[stachka-v-laerte|more details]]). Guards beat Fernan; Alvaro intervenes with his revolver, breaks one thug’s nose, and is struck with a rifle butt in the elbow and stomach. Two cannon volleys sound, both blanks, and everyone flees. At home, Alvaro finds a woman’s severed finger in his notebook of notes about Juanes, along with a message: “Stop this!”

He goes to the police station. The alguacil [[diego-ingres|Diego Ingres]] explains that the law is powerless: speech is free, a verbal threat is not punishable, and only the finger’s owner can bring a claim. Alvaro sinks into creative apathy: for three days he refuses to answer the door, shoots at his rivals’ names in the yard, tries to throw away his bracelet, lashes out at Malena (she throws the bracelet after him), and writes to Filippe rejecting the commission. Only Fernan, after sorting through his mail, pulls his friend out of depression: Alvaro agrees to write a chapter about Maduro. At the manufacturer’s mansion, he is received by the guard Salvador rather than the owner. Alvaro improvises a speech about justice and demands that only one of Craft’s three allens be a manufacturer. When the guards try to detain him, he reminds them that holding a person on white land is a crime, and leaves.

## The exhibition, the philosophy of horror, and the stranger (ch. 16)
Filippe summons Alvaro to the [[galereya-eliste|Eliste Gallery]] for the exhibition “The Philosophy of Horror.” Refusing to accept his withdrawal, he reprimands him harshly: what is tormenting him does not matter; whether he has written the book does. Afterward, Alvaro goes to [[khose-flarbo|Jose Flarbo’s]] shop for a book by the philosopher [[liess-van-khalm|van Halm]] and meets an intelligent stranger in a gray dress, who buys his first novel. They argue about his heroines, and she writes an address and a time on the flyleaf; she does not give her name.

## The loss of Isabel and the wound (ch. 18–20)
Inspiration returns: Alvaro formulates four questions — how to kill on white land, what sort of doctor Juanes was, what the poison was and who made it, and how to live under threats — and takes them to Carlos. Carlos lies drunk in a dark room: Isabel was abducted outside the gallery after that very exhibition. Alvaro turns cold, because the finger in his cellar could have belonged to her, but says nothing. They drink together, and Carlos explains that Juanes’s loose tongue killed him, and that “it began with da Vira and ended with this.”

On his way out of the Old Port, Alvaro is attacked by a stranger, who strikes his jaw with brass knuckles, says in a hoarse voice, “You were told to stop,” and shoots through his right palm. Alvaro manages to reach the Three Bulls, where [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan]] and [[lyusil|Lucille]] bandage him. There he meets [[mor|Mor]] and her assistant, [[malysh|Little One]]. Mor describes her plan: to secure another debate with the manufacturers, which requires taking Fatima’s lawyers out of the contest. Alvaro believes they want him to seduce Fatima and leaves in anger. Outside, he meets Beatriz, shows her the wound, and they reconcile. Beatriz becomes his driver and assistant, while his wound forces him to write with a scribe’s help.

## Theories and the Great Scroll (ch. 22)
Alvaro and Beatriz develop a theory involving two adversaries: a “Kaiser’s Claw,” a Dangarian spy, and a young avenger, supposedly the son of Fatima and the late navigator [[red|Rodrigo da Vira]]. He first looks for confirmation in the Great Scroll, but finds only records of da Vira’s hot temper, his posthumous award, and the unsuccessful claim brought by [[karmensita-estevos|Carmensita Estevos]]. Beatriz finds only that Fatima has a daughter. For the time being, this trail leads nowhere.

## Meetings with the stranger (ch. 24–26)
For their first meeting, the stranger takes Alvaro to a teaching hospital for a public autopsy: she wants to test his mettle. He runs out twice, overcome with nausea, but the words “tamponade” and “rupture of the heart” suggest the solution to the mystery. The next day, he takes Beatriz to Juanes’s house and reconstructs the day of his death: the doctor mistakenly drank someone else’s medicine, while the emergency vial contained bonk, which the butler Emil had poured into an empty bottle. Alvaro remarks to the butler that the mystery would never have attracted his attention if Emil had not removed the black ribbons from the little tree, and places his fate in Beatriz’s hands. An entry in the patient register leads him to van Halm.

At the [[klinika-miloserdiya|Mercy Clinic]], the philosopher can neither speak nor write, but responds to the names of Solanta’s heroes and to the word “schönbrun,” and cowers in a corner in terror at the word “Eliga.” That evening, Alvaro leads the stranger through a secret passage in the Bridge Bastion into a former royal wine cellar, now a restaurant. They discuss the approaching election of the supremo, then hide among the barrels from unexpected visitors and kiss.

## Fatima, Beatriz, and Fernan (ch. 28–30)
In the morning, Alvaro studies the woman’s bedroom like a detective and concludes that she is successful and hardworking. Then he listens at the keyhole as General Deserey visits and calls her “ma donna.” This is how Alvaro discovers that the stranger is [[fatima|Fatima Ir-Reya]], prim-allen of Law. In anger, he says da Vira’s name aloud, and Fatima throws him out. Outside, Beatriz’s Esperanza almost runs him over, and the dyed roots of her hair give him the answer: she is Fatima’s daughter. The wayward “Unoko Ir-Reya” he read about in the Scroll is a fiction she invented about herself. He calls her a lying coward and leaves.

In chapter 30, it emerges that Fernan also knew about Beatriz’s parentage: Mor has sent Alvaro a letter written in his presence by Fernan disguised as a street copyist. Alvaro hits his friend with his injured hand. Fernan admits that he wanted contact with Fatima for the movement. “Since everyone is using me, it is my turn to use everyone,” Alvaro decides. He goes to Deserey, extracts a thousand escudos from the general with a story about a Dangarian spy, and, although he does not believe in the spy himself, brings up “The Philosophy of Horror”; the general flinches. In a public garden, Alvaro reaches his conclusion: the heroes of Solanta are secretly connected to one another and to van Halm’s book; the philosopher saw something in the Well that he wrote about to Fatima and Juanes. He decides to travel to Selezia. At the Three Bulls, his palm is stitched up, and before he leaves Lucille assures him that Selezia is the dullest of places.

## Tres Molinos and Last Winter (ch. 32–34)
In Tres Molinos, the friends read Deserey’s diaries, from which the names of Solanta’s heroes have disappeared. Their hired guide, Javier Salam, and Senora Marques show them around van Halm’s castle, and Alvaro notices peculiarities: the bedroom door is unlocked, and the food is kept in the cellar. He finds a lead: the Dangarian servants hired by the philosopher shortly before his downfall. In the street, Alvaro recognizes Doña Isabel, whom everyone believed dead, in a passing woman: she ran away from her husband with a lover. She explains that her husband had turned her into a possession and allowed her neither letters nor meetings, and that the Well showed her a vine strangling a flower — so she fled.

That evening in the castle, which Alvaro calls “Last Winter,” he exposes the secret of the “cursed” door: the night had been so cold that the lubricant thickened and the lock jammed from within. He reenacts the scene using a sack of ice. His second deduction is that Rodrigo da Vira is alive and taking revenge himself: the doge sold him into Dangarian slavery, the navigator found companions, and he returned to Catalia. Then two killers murder Deserey’s agents, take Beatriz hostage, and force Alvaro to talk. They are [[salvador|Salvador]], a Dangarian spy, and an unnamed gunman. In the cellar beside the dark schönbrun, Alvaro and his friends lay a trap: Fernan blinds Salvador with a lamp, Alvaro finishes him off and wounds the second killer, and then Fatima arrives with [[frej|Frey]] and ten alguacils. For details, see [[posledniaya-zima-shturm|“The Night at Last Winter”]] and [[posledniaya-zima|“Last Winter”]]. Fatima explains that she never had an affair with da Vira and that she wrote the anonymous letter to Juanes herself. She asks Alvaro and Beatriz “not to become too dear to her”: according to the laws of misfortune, calamity strikes what is dearest. Alvaro answers with a kiss.

## Relationships (ch. 0–34)
- [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan Gereira]] — a loyal friend, secretary, and scribe; he concealed Beatriz’s identity from Alvaro and received a punch in the face for it, but stayed by his side.
- [[lyusil|Lucille Gereira]] — a friend who feeds Alvaro when he has no money.
- [[beatris|Beatriz]] — his student, driver, and assistant; she later turns out to be Fatima’s daughter. Alvaro grows attached to her, but continues arguing fiercely with her until the final chapter.
- [[fatima|Fatima Ir-Reya]] — his lover, whom he first knew as a stranger and then as the prim-allen of Law. At the end, they travel to Laerta together.
- [[filippe|Filippe di Berro]] — the patron who commissions his book and a stern mentor.
- [[malena|Malena]] — his former lover, a café owner; Alvaro quarrels with her when he loses faith in himself.
- [[karlos|Carlos el Niña]], [[dezerej|Deserey]], [[kristobal-maduro|Maduro]] — the heroes of his book; each turns out to be connected with the secret of Solanta.
- [[vinsente-ramires|Vincente Ramires]] — his opponent in the dueling debate; see his article for details.
- [[mor|Mor]] and the Reformists — allies through circumstance; Alvaro does not share their views, but defends the vulnerable.

## Conception and interviews
According to the author, Alvaro arose from a desire to explore creativity in fantasy, where artist protagonists are almost absent. The author acknowledges that Alvaro is to some extent a projection of himself, an “alter ego,” and that the character’s philosophy largely matches his own; as an example, he cited the idea that someone who can write rarely teaches others. At the same time, he warned beginning writers against a protagonist who resembles them too closely. The author calls Alvaro one of the novel’s three main characters; the other two are not people of the arts.

The interviews also state that Alvaro is writing the book Traces on the Water, meaning that the novel itself is structured as a book within a book. The author said that he checked the autopsy scene with the help of AI as a “doctor friend.” Asked about the stress in the name, the author said that “Alvara do Silva” was correct, adding the qualification that he had only just begun learning Spanish.

## At the end of the novel (ch. 34)
By the ending, Alvaro is alive but wounded in the hand, ribs, and neck. He has solved the mystery of the “curse,” killed Salvador, and survived the fight. The book about the heroes of Solanta remains unfinished, and the gold bracelet and place on the Scroll remain dreams. He travels to Laerta with Fatima; when he speaks of their long-awaited meeting, she replies, “That is tomorrow’s Fatima’s problem.” The novel does not say what happens next.
