Elsa von Walbrecht is a baroness and owner of the Beautiful Well, one of Hofstadt’s schönbruns. Her mansion on the Wunde is styled after a steamship. She hosts an annual fishing party “for select society,” is a friend of Master [[joriksen|Fritjof Joriksen]], and knows how to smooth over any conflict at her table.

## The house and the well (ch. 14–21)
The mansion stands in northern Hofstadt between the docks and the railway bridge. It has three stories, granite columns shaped like steamship funnels, windows resembling cabins, and a paddle wheel turning in the pond to the right of the entrance. The novel explains that the house was built here because the schönbrun is here; the palace is merely a handsome accessory. The well is hidden in a round, white pavilion with stained-glass windows, resembling a conservatory. Dense parkland surrounds it, liveried guards carry double-barreled guns, and girls in glasses stand watch at the entrance. Inside the pavilion is a small theater with velvet chairs, where spectators look down into the well through mirrors on the ceiling. In chapter 21, Joriksen tells Dietrich that the baroness’s schönbrun likes products of prime numbers, hence the thirty-five guests and fifteen varieties of wine.

## Character and appearance
Elsa is beautiful and considerably older than Dietrich. The narrator observes that she “could have been Olkha’s aunt, not her sister.” She treats guests informally and asks to be called simply Frau Elsa or madam. She is kind and tactful; the narrator calls her the kind baroness. She knows how to defuse conflicts, although she herself charges two hundred marks for admission to the fishing party, and a millionaire merchant at the table says she should have asked for a thousand.

## The fishing and reception (ch. 10–14)
Dietrich learns about the fishing from the Society Herald in chapter 10: its theme will be “ailments of the heart in every sense.” In chapter 14 ([[rybalka-valbrekht|Fishing at Baroness Walbrecht’s]]), the baroness herself finds Dietrich among the guests, seats him, and introduces Joriksen onstage. According to Joriksen’s sea reckoning, there are thirty-six guests instead of thirty-five, and the master considers Dietrich the “extra.” The baroness refuses to send him away and says she would sooner leave herself. At the festive table, she introduces the guests: Joriksen, the newspaper proprietor [[varkhajt|Count von Wahrheit]], and Prince [[lents|Wladyslaw Lenz]] with his niece [[olkha|Olkha]].

Later, in a gazebo, Dietrich finds Joriksen and Elsa in serious conversation: the master speaks of people killed to the last person, a bewildered guild, and the fact that Glory has already reported it. The master and the baroness address each other familiarly. Dietrich tells them about his schönbrun in Ollandia, and the master exchanges a glance with Elsa; she nods almost imperceptibly: “I believe him.”

## Olkha’s patron (ch. 14–25)
The prince asks Elsa to take Olkha under her protection, and in chapter 17 the baroness invites the Lenzes to stay with her. She seeks a suitable husband for his niece: the prince tells Count Lurd that the baroness has suggested a duke’s grandson and a margrave’s son. Elsa is concerned about scandal, and in chapter 25 she implores the prince not to fight a duel, appealing to the young people’s infatuation. She does not expose Dietrich, although she knows he is von Dort rather than von Eyring.

## The morning after the scandal (ch. 29)
During the negotiations between Lurd and Lenz, Elsa offers the prince schnapps and dinner. Once fed, the prince becomes more amenable. Otto and Milosh appear at the gate. The baroness orders them admitted: “These days, whenever someone goes out somewhere, trouble begins.” The porter at her gate is a stone; he remembers Milosh carrying notes for the princess.

## A shared surname (ch. 31)
At Field Marshal [[rajniger|Reiniger’s]] headquarters, a corps commander, Major General Walbrecht, is mentioned as being assigned to lead four divisions south. The novel gives no connection to the baroness.
