Milosh is a stone slave and orderly to Lieutenant [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort]]. He is almost everywhere Dietrich is: on the hill behind the lines in [[ollandiya|Ollandia]], in the scenes surrounding the Claws’ interrogation, in the capital, the hotel, and the train. In every scene, he serves as a second voice for his master — practical, stubborn, and direct. Dietrich calls him his “comrade-in-arms” and even trusts him with a pistol and money.

## Character and appearance

Milosh talks freely and willingly, with the air of a man who knows life: when Dietrich remarks that he has “dressed himself up as a wise man,” he replies that he has simply seen life, speaks of what he knows, and keeps silent about what he does not. He consoles his master and argues with him, likes tea and schnapps, and takes every opportunity to drink with Dietrich and instruct him. He cannot read, which he calmly admits himself (ch. 25). His appearance is barely described; all that is known is that the color drains from his face when frightened.

Dietrich tolerates his insolence and himself points out that he has sent Milosh into town and to the station, with money and with a pistol, yet he has never tried to escape. Mari, however, sees the orderly as an unwanted extra person in the house.

## At the battery (ch. 2–6)

In ch. 2, Milosh sits with Dietrich in the tent on the hill, argues that the battery has not been forgotten, and praises his master as a young eagle worthy of battle. On the morning after the stone platoon’s escape, he is the first to hear a horse neighing in the woods and turns pale on realizing that Ollandian cavalry is nearby. Dietrich sends him uphill to raise the alarm, while he himself goes to the well with the holy-fool stone [[spasenie|Salvation]]. Milosh does not see everything that happens below, at the well.

In ch. 6, in [[latsen|Latsen]], he runs to fetch his master when Major [[klyajn|Klein]] summons him. The conversation with the Claw takes place without him, but Dietrich’s account mentions him as the orderly the lieutenant sent to raise the alarm at the battery.

## In Hofstadt (ch. 10–21)

In ch. 10, Milosh arrives home with Dietrich. Mari refuses to let the dirty orderly into the house, and Frau [[gizeke|Gieseke]] houses him in her servants’ room, where, at her request, he is to entertain her stones with war stories. He recounts the final battle to the Frau herself: how the commander died and the lieutenant immediately assumed command. After the quarrel, Dietrich and Milosh get drunk in a tavern, and Milosh expounds the philosophy of schnapps: a wife can control her husband with tears and reproaches, but she has no power over schnapps. He also reveals that he was married, but Dietrich’s father bought him and assigned him to his son, while his wife remained with the previous owner. He never asked for her to be bought from her owner, to avoid receiving “a gift in female form”; now that the money is gone, he can speak of it without risk. Later, drunk, he spends the night in Dietrich’s apartment, provoking another quarrel: Mari grabs Heinz and threatens to leave until Dietrich gives in.

In ch. 14, Mari initially suspects Milosh of stealing their savings and asks her husband to question him. In ch. 17, he meets his returning master on the stairs, passes on Mari’s message that “he had better not even try coming in,” and admits that he invented a “governor-general” for Frau Gieseke, with whom the lieutenant supposedly stayed late. After a week of searching the archives, he has failed to find the names of the former owners of the land where Dietrich “displayed heroism”: registers in the conquered territories are being reformed. In ch. 21, he again unsuccessfully searches for the owner of the land containing the schönbrun and moves into hotel rooms with Dietrich.

## Milosh and Killnot (ch. 33)

Milosh knows the fusilier platoon only from ch. 2. In ch. 33, the rifleman Killnot, who escaped that night, is revealed to be [[red|Red]]; Milosh does not learn this in the novel.

## Letters to Olkha and departure (ch. 25–29)

In ch. 25, Milosh carries Dietrich’s letters to Princess [[olkha|Olkha]] and swears that he gave both to the butler. He refuses to carry a fourth letter: the porter has already threatened to beat him. Dietrich then orders him to watch Baroness [[elza|Elsa’s]] house, and Milosh befriends the porter and learns that important guests will visit the Lenzes the next day. His observations that the princess will be at home, presentably dressed, and among important guests sound like advice to go and explain matters, although, as he says himself, papers and invitations are “not his department.”

In ch. 29, he arrives at the baroness’s house with [[otto|Otto]] at Dietrich’s most difficult moment and whispers that the fischmeister demands immediate departure: “everyone has died.” Dietrich passes this off as news of losses in his company, and Prince [[lents|Lenz]] lets him leave. On the train, Milosh goes to the dining car to arrange breakfast.

## Relationships

- **[[ditrikh|Dietrich]]** — his master; Dietrich’s father bought Milosh and assigned him to his son. Dietrich calls him a comrade-in-arms, while Milosh takes a sober view of his master’s weaknesses.
- **[[mari|Mari]]** — to her, Milosh is an unwanted extra person in the household and the first suspect.
- **[[gizeke|Frau Gieseke]]** treats him kindly, offers refreshments, and gives him a place to sleep.

## Conception and interviews

In one interview, the host called Milosh a likable character and suggested that his Slavic name indicates that he is not Dangarian. The author did not confirm this in the recording, and the novel itself does not specify Milosh’s origins; according to the author, Slavic names are more characteristic of Ollandians.

## At the end of the novel (ch. 29)

Milosh is last seen on the way to Ollandia in ch. 29. In ch. 31, when Dietrich is arrested at Latsen station, the orderly is not mentioned, and the novel does not say what happens to him afterward.
