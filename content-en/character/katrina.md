Katrina von Lurd is a young countess of the Dangarian nobility: von Eyring by birth, von Lurd by marriage. She is Olkha’s friend from boarding school and mistress of the house in Schlissel where Dietrich pretends to be her cousin. Katrina is cheerful, decisive, and secretive. She knows just when to play along with someone else’s story and knows how to escape a dangerous situation, but her secret forces her to keep her “brother” close.

## Name and origins (ch. 17–31)
Katrina is called by two surnames in the novel, and both are genuine. Her maiden name is Katrina von Eyring, from the family whose name is borne by the town of [[ejring|Eyring]]; in chapter 31, she introduces herself to the major as “Katrina von Lurd, née von Eyring.” Her parents chose her husband: Count Ludwig von Lurd proposed after being struck by her beauty, and they agreed at once. She is called “one of those very Eyrings,” and cannot stand the phrase “those very.” Her kinsman and head of the family is Margrave Maximian von Eyring, “stingy with everyone, but protective of his own.” The novel does not specify whether he is her father, uncle, or a more distant relative.

When they first met, Olkha did not know whose daughter Katrina was: she introduced herself simply as “Katrina,” and her friend learned her surname and title only later.

## Character and appearance
A tall, fair-haired “eagle” woman with blue eyes set too far apart. Dietrich describes her as a “spirited girl”; at boarding school she quickly declared Olkha her best friend and “would accept no objections.” At the graduation ball in Schlissel, she shone and had no shortage of suitors.

Observing her, Major Klein notes that she is charismatic and unable to conceal her emotions. She enjoys practical jokes: her stories of “childhood with Dietrich” feature tar in shoes, perfume replaced with Almagarian bonk, and a monologue delivered to the servants rather than onstage.

## Hostess to the “siblings” (ch. 17)
On Saturday, Olkha brings Dietrich to her, having passed him off to her uncle as “Dietrich von Eyring.” Katrina greets them, calls him her “dear little brother,” and reminds him of a primer covered in ink. She warns him that her husband will take him into the study, so he must sound confident, whatever nonsense he says, and ask for valisyan. At dinner, she takes up the invented childhood story and adds one of her own. The count quickly catches himself and leaves to sort correspondence, signaling to his wife that no more such stories are needed. She kisses her friend and her “brother” on the cheek.

Olkha’s remarks make clear that Prince [[lents|Lenz]] dislikes Katrina. He feels that she has spoiled his plans, since she married a man such as Lurd while Olkha failed to do so.

## Olkha’s bedroom (ch. 25)
In autumn, Katrina and the count visit Baroness [[elza|Elsa’s]] mansion, where Olkha is staying. Katrina says she came uninvited to see her friend. At the table she complains of a migraine, asks her husband’s permission to leave, and Olkha goes to see her off.

Dietrich waits seven minutes and finds Olkha’s room. He sees Olkha in a dressing gown, scattered clothing, and Katrina on the bed in her underwear. Katrina does not lose her head: she tears Dietrich’s shirt and scratches his skin, throws her belongings out of the window, and jumps after them. This turns an accidental encounter into a scandal over the princess’s “seduction.” The novel does not explicitly state her motives. Dietrich later accuses her of having “seduced” the princess; the narrator calls Katrina an “adulteress” with a secret that could send three people to the block.

## Witness and ally (ch. 29)
In the morning, Katrina is summoned to the baroness’s house. She pretends to be suffering from a migraine and tells her husband and the prince a convenient version: she introduced the young people while they were still studying; she calls Dietrich a “fledgling,” assures them that the von Eyrings will defend him staunchly, and promises to visit Margrave Maximian. She does not reveal his real surname: if she speaks, Dietrich will reveal her secret, and both will lose their heads. Unexpectedly, she changes the conversation herself, asking what dowry Olkha will receive and thereby outraging the prince. When her husband demands assurances from Dietrich, she feeds him the words, reinforcing their shared lie.

## Freeing Dietrich (ch. 31)
Later, in Latsen, Katrina and Olkha burst in on Major [[klyajn|Klein]]. She gives her name, demands to see the arrest warrant, and, when none is produced, orders the major and the gendarme to stand because “you are speaking to a countess.” She then throws the visiting cards of Count Ludwig von Lurd and Margrave von Eyring onto the desk. Understanding the power of these connections, Klein apologizes, and her “brother” is released. Katrina tells the major that men like him are a disgrace to the empire.

Klein tests whether the young women know about the schönbrun near the station: Katrina does not react to his hint. He concludes that they do not.

## Relationships
- [[lurd|Ludwig von Lurd]] — her husband, about fifty years old.
- [[olkha|Olkha]] — her best friend; Katrina helps her and comes to her rescue.
- [[ditrikh|Dietrich]] — her “brother” in the cover story, a “fledgling.”
- Margrave Maximian von Eyring — the head of her family, whose authority she invokes.

## Conception and interviews
According to the author in the first stream, among the Dangarian characters he likes Count Lurd and his wife Katrina, Master Joriksen, and, to a lesser extent, Dietrich. The transcript is inaccurate with names, so this is a paraphrase rather than a quotation.
