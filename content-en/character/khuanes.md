Garcia Juanes is a physician who dies before the novel's main action begins. He was the ship's doctor on the [[solantijskaya-ekspeditsiya|Solantan Expedition]] and is considered one of the five heroes about whom [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]] must write a book. Prim-Allen [[filippe|Filippe di Berro]] adds his name to the list with a black eyebrow pencil, saying that he died the day before yesterday and is indifferent to fame. The mystery of his death is the first that Alvaro solves.

## Physician and Man (ch. 5–18)
Juanes returned from Solanta a hero and quickly built a lucrative practice: officers, dons, and allens consulted him. According to Emil, he treated patients neither better nor worse than others, but he knew how to listen. His only “discoveries” were remedies that altered blood pressure; Juanes himself had hypertension and took a balsam from a blue vial. He lived on the edge of the Valentian Gardens, in the white district near the former royal palace.

According to the butler, the widow considered her husband a truthful man. She recounts how he took a mistress, warned his wife in advance that it would last three months, and returned on the 91st day; he also assured his son that he would make a poor doctor. This is the doctor as presented by his family. Alvaro forms a different opinion in ch. 9: he chatted with patients instead of working, was rude to his wife, children, and noble patients, frightened people with the severity of their illnesses, and then “cured” them. Carlos el Niña says he was an ordinary physician who “talked too much,” disliked by both him and Fatima (ch. 18).

## Death and the First Investigation (ch. 5–9)
The butler Emil describes the final day: the doctor saw his regular patients, and after lunch an important artillery officer visited him. Juanes prescribed medicine for low blood pressure and ordered tea. Absorbed in the guest's account of the Kaiser's victory over the Ollandian princes, he burned his mouth on hot tea, broke his cup, and snatched the officer's glass. By evening, he felt unwell; he took his balsam, then another dose, and went up to the roof for fresh air, but collapsed on the stairs. The doctors' conclusion was a rupture of the heart muscle.

[[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] inspects the house with [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan Gereira]] and secretly takes an anonymous note assembled from newspaper words: “I know everything. You will not escape retribution.” From the patient ledger, he notices that [[dezerej|General Deserey]] visited the doctor on the day of his death, although the general claimed he had not seen any of his companions for six months (ch. 9). Fernan suggests the note came from a mistress; Alvaro believes the doctor had a secret.

## Threats and Theories (ch. 11–22)
The investigation temporarily recedes into the background: Alvaro receives a severed finger with a note saying “Stop this!”, then is shot through the palm. He connects the doctor's death to [[izabel|Isabel el Niña's]] disappearance, the Philosophy of Horror, and the sailor da Vira, whose name Carlos lets slip. In ch. 22, Alvaro works through possible poisons—none in the reference book fits—and constructs a theory involving revenge by da Vira's son.

## The Death Explained (ch. 24)
At a public autopsy in the teaching hospital, Alvaro hears the terms “tamponade” and “rupture of the heart muscle” for the first time and suddenly understands what happened. Together with [[beatris|Beatriz]], he reconstructs the day of the death. The doctor had prescribed Deserey drops of his red preparation for low blood pressure; the general had not taken it immediately. Distracted, Juanes mistakenly drank the measured drops from the general's glass: in a man with hypertension, they triggered an attack. He sent his wife for the blue balsam, but the butler, left alone, had poured some bonk received as a gift into an empty vial to taste it and had not had time to put it away. The wife took that very vial. The doctor drank the strong bonk without tasting it because of his burned mouth, and his heart gave out.

Thus Emil killed Juanes without knowing it, which is why the taboo of [[belaya-zemlya|white land]] did not take effect. Emil admits removing the black ribbons from the [[sinkho|sinkho]] tree to conceal the death. Beatriz forgives him in exchange for information about van Halm (see below). See also [[smert-khuanesa|The Death of Dr. Juanes]].

## Juanes and van Halm (ch. 24–26)
According to Emil, three months before his death, Juanes received a letter from the philosopher [[liess-van-khalm|Liess van Halm]] and went to visit him. He returned displeased: “a very strange man, possibly mental illness.” Three weeks later, a chauffeur with uneven shoulders, sent by Don [[kristobal-maduro|Maduro]], arrived at night and took him away. Returning the following evening, the doctor wrote: “Liess van Halm, 49, schizophrenia, transferred to the [[klinika-miloserdiya|Mercy Clinic]].” From then on, his blood pressure often rose, and he refused to discuss the philosopher. The Mercy Clinic confirms that Juanes brought the philosopher there together with a chauffeur (ch. 26).

## Role in the Heroes' Story (ch. 28–34)
In conversation with Fatima, General Deserey says that “after what happened to the doctor,” they cannot wage war; Alvaro overhears the phrase and later, in ch. 30, regards it as evidence of secret connections between the heroes. In ch. 34, Alvaro explains that Juanes diagnosed the navigator Rodrigo da Vira with incurable sarcoma of the lungs and told the captain, which became a link in the chain of da Vira's betrayal. Fatima adds that she herself sent Juanes the anonymous note: after receiving da Vira's letter, she visited the doctor to ask how a “terminally ill” man could write to them years later; Juanes insisted the diagnosis had been correct, and in a rage she assembled the note from newspaper words. Alvaro sums it up as “the doctor died from an elixir of his own making,” although in reality bonk had replaced the contents of the final vial.

## Conception and Interviews
There are no explicit comments by the author about Juanes in the available interviews. In one stream, the interviewer remarked that the chapter about the doctor's house makes it clear that “things are not so simple”—this was the interviewer's conjecture, not the author's statement.
