The article has two parts. **The author’s words** covers what Roman Surzhikov himself named as models in two streams ([[slovo-avtora|The Author’s Words]]). **Readers’ observations** covers parallels evident in the text that the author has not confirmed; these should not be treated as his statements.

## The author’s words

### Spain and Catalia
In the spring stream, the author said that Catalian is Spanish, and that Catalia’s style, naming system, and clothing were “copied” from Spain. But this borrowing is superficial: “one cannot say that Catalia is Spain.” According to him, the country’s social order is completely unlike Spain’s; he first devised the state and only afterward “applied a skin” of Spanish style. He chose that style partly because he had come to love Spanish romance and began learning Spanish while conceiving the world. See [[kataliya|Catalia]], [[kultura-katalii|Catalian Culture]].

In terms of ideas, the author defined Catalia as a “triumph of materialism” and a society of individualists, where everyone is occupied with personal success and the dream of rising from obrero to maestro and don resembles the American dream. Religion there, he says, was never unified: there was once “creepy Lovecraftian paganism,” which the First Judge later abolished.

### Germany and Dangar
Dangarian is German, and its style—ranks, clothing, kaiser—is German-Prussian, but saying “Dangar is Prussia” would be wrong. Like Catalia, the country is an “embodiment of certain ideas and principles of the state.” The author described its period as a notional “X century”: steam engines, cannons, and slavery existing simultaneously. See [[dangar|Dangar]], [[kultura-dangara|Dangarian Culture]].

### The Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth and Ollandia
The host observed that Ollandia resembles Poland and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth in both style and its sad fate; the author replied: “in places, yes, there is a resemblance.” The Ollandians’ language is “more or less” Polish, although the author had not thought it through. Their names are Slavic, and he selected them to sound different from Dangarian names. See [[ollandiya|Ollandia]], [[kultura-ollandii|Ollandia and the Ollandians]].

### Lovecraft and the Ancients
The author confirmed the resemblance between Catalia’s Ancients and Lovecraftian gods with the word “Lovecraft”: the sea monsters worshipped before the First Judge survive in curses, art, and local color, but do not interfere in life. See [[drevnie|Ancients]].

### Polari and the spiral
According to the author, the World of Wells is another turn of the same “universal spiral” on which the world of “Polari” lies. It is impossible to move physically from one world to the other. He himself named the sole direct reference to “Polari”: a military acquaintance whose criticisms of military scenes inspired the figure of General Deserey. See [[otsylki-k-polari|References to Polari]].

### Ideas matter more than models
The author’s general principle is not to copy a state from a single real-world model, but to invent its order and only then clothe it in familiar imagery. He chooses names “for a range of reasons,” including making sure that they do not resemble one another, rather than for a historical allusion.

## Readers’ observations
The following are parallels visible in the text itself. The author has not commented on them.

### Names and language
Catalian names and titles have a Spanish structure—da Silva, di Berro, el Niña, don, doña, maestro—while Dangarian ones are German: von Dort, Hofmeyer, Kurtzleben, Herr, Frau. Ollandian names are Slavic: Olkha, Wladyslaw, Milosh. The names of “Dangarian” places are transparent to someone who knows German: Hofstadt means “court city,” Schlissel (German Schlüssel) means “key,” Witwen means “widows”; there are the Kobold Sea, Dunkwald, “dark forest,” schönbrun, “beautiful well,” and Täuschung, “deception.” The word “schönbrun” coincides with the name of Vienna’s Schönbrunn Palace. More details: [[yazyki|Languages]].

### Technology and period
Catalia has steam locomotives, steam carriages, revolvers, breech-loading carbines, and howitzers; Dangar fights with flintlock muskets, six-pounder cannons, and mortars, but has railways and airships. Catalian technology is close to that of mid-nineteenth-century Europe, while the Dangarian army’s weapons are closer to the eighteenth century. Its ranks—staff captain, lieutenant, feldwebel, noncommissioned officer—are familiar from the Imperial Russian Army. More details: [[tekhnika|Technology]].

### Place names and features of the world
- A coastal republic with opera, rice and wine, romances, olive groves, and steppes where bulls graze (ch. 4, 9) brings Mediterranean Spain to mind.
- The name “Catalia” recalls Spain’s Catalonia; the novel does not discuss this connection.
- The Muari Emirates, ruled by a sultan with a harem and competing with Catalia in maritime trade, recall medieval Moorish-Spanish relations. The name “Muari” resembles “Moors.” The island of Solanta, with its ruler called a doge, recalls Venice and its doge.
- The esferas and their guild districts partly resemble medieval urban guilds; but the author explicitly said that the country’s structure is not Spanish, and named no specific model.
- A “dueling debate” replaces a fight with a verbal contest before jurors, a method familiar from courtroom rhetoric.

### Religion and imagery (ch. 8)
Dangarian faith is connected with the Gospel image of the Sower; the prohibition “thou shalt not kill” is burned into a soldier’s chest with a red-hot chain; icons depict suffering and sacrifice in the spirit of Christian iconography. Slave priests and feeding by hand have no exact parallel among these comparisons and belong specifically to the World of Wells.

### The book within the book (ch. 34)
The device in which the writer-protagonist turns out to be the author of the very book being read is called a “book within a book”; that is how the stream’s host described it. The author confirmed only that Alvaro does indeed write “Traces on the Water.” See [[sledy-na-vode|Traces on the Water]].
