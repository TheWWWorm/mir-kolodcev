Stones are one of Dangar’s three estates, alongside eagles and hares ([[orly]]). In everyday speech, they are called “stones,” or “Steins” in Dangarian. Every stone is supposed to wear a small stone on a chain around the neck, a sign of faith in the [[seyatel|Sower]] and of “God’s love and care”; many also have a brand burned into their forearms. Stones perform almost all the empire’s menial labor and form the bulk of its army, but according to church doctrine they have no free will or choice: thus they are relieved of the burden of answering for their actions.

## Their place in society (ch. 2–6)
In the Sower’s parable, the stones were the first to answer his call and move aside, revealing clear soil. Therefore, scripture says, they are “cherished with love” more than the others: God has freed them from responsibility for their actions, the agony of choosing, and the uncertainty of finding their path and place in life (ch. 2, 6). Dietrich von Dort uses this argument in a dispute with his soldiers: if the stones have already been saved, there is no need to save them.

The empire rests on the stones. Almost all its goods are made by their hands; in an interview, the author stressed that the higher estates take care of the stones and that the system is tightly interconnected—if the stones were freed, “everything would fall apart.” He also said that the stones won the privilege of “being fed by hand” for themselves, and promised the detailed history in later books.

## Daily life (ch. 4–33)
- **Settlements.** Slaves are housed in dugouts resembling a network of mushrooms, where they live without guards: there is nowhere to escape to (ch. 4). A foreman is appointed to head each dugout; he too is a slave, but receives better bunks and food and stands above the others (ch. 4, 8).
- **Food.** The law feeds only those who work: a sick or crippled slave is given no food (ch. 12). The exception is the church’s “feeding,” when free people spoon-feed the stones.
- **Punishments.** Sentences are determined by law; lashes are administered on the altar in the temple, and the blood is rubbed into the stone (ch. 12). The usual execution is a bullet in the back of the head on the altar stone; the soul is believed to fly to the Sower (ch. 33).
- **Brand and stone.** A digger released from slavery is made to wear a stone while digging (ch. 23). Church slaves receive a brand bearing the Sower’s name, and if it heals completely, the slave may become a priest (ch. 33). Dangar’s priesthood is recruited mainly from the stones (ch. 25).

## The army and the law (ch. 2, 6, 19, 33)
The great majority of the army’s rank and file are stones. In ch. 2, Dietrich’s battery is manned by slave fusiliers; only the noncommissioned officer Gelb is free. The escape of an entire platoon of stones in ch. 2 is unheard of, and Major Klein builds his case against Dietrich around it. Dunkwald’s guards are also branded slaves armed with muskets (ch. 19). The army’s head levy requires slave owners to surrender one stone in every hundred each year (ch. 33).

A person may be enslaved for debt: a debtor who is not noble hands over a loved one to become a stone (ch. 21). Joriksen inserts such collateral into the pledge involving Dietrich’s son Heinz. If the empire considers the nobility of a conquered country unreliable—“unsicher”—between twenty and seventy percent of its population is branded as slaves (ch. 29).

## Well diggers (ch. 4–33)
Stone slaves, like Red himself, dig wells for the Fishermen’s Guild. Each has a personal Zustand ([[tsushtand]]), and the stones endure different forms of suffering, from digging with bare hands to self-torment. The estate supplies the guild with “material” for its experiment in creating a schönbrun ([[kolodets]]).

## Each stone in their own way (ch. 2–33)
Among the stones, the book singles out the good-natured soldier [[spasenie|Salvation]] with his faith in “the water”; the foreman who hates Red ([[brigadir]]); [[shults|red-haired Schultz]], who has lost his toes; [[tot-bez-nogtej|The One Without Nails]], who digs a well with his bare hands; [[boroda-lopatoj|Spadebeard]]; and the young scribe ([[pisarenok]]), for whom a career as a fischmeister is predicted. Red is initially considered a stone, but he has no brand and no stone around his neck: he is a “foreigner,” and this matters to the plot.

## The concept and interviews
According to the author, Dangar incorporates other peoples by enslaving some of their peasants as stones, turning the middle class into hares, and their nobles into eagles. He gave [[ollandiya|Ollandia]] as an example: there is no slavery there, and its population can easily be fitted into the Dangarian system.
