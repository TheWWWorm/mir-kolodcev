**Alguacils** are Catalian keepers of order from the esfera of Law. The narrator explains in ch. 13: “Two kinds of people keep order in Catalia: guards and alguacils.”

## Guards and alguacils (ch. 13)
The two services work together, but are organized differently.

- **Guards** belong to the [[teni|esfera of Shadow]]. They wear thick beards but shave their mustaches to make their jaws look “heavy as a bulldog’s”; they do not wear hats, and tie their hair with red scarves in pirate fashion. They carry all sorts of weapons: clubs, parrying daggers, guns, and pistols. Guards intervene when there is no doubt that a crime has been committed: a fight somewhere fighting is forbidden, violence against a woman without a prostitute’s bracelet, or a Shadow master exceeding the authority of his guild.
- **Alguacils** belong to the esfera of Law. They take on a case when it is necessary to determine how far the letter of the [[svitok|Scroll]] has been violated, who is responsible—and whether there was a crime at all. They carry the latest weapons, carbines and revolvers, but regard their minds as their chief weapon. They wear pointed beards and mustaches “that give the face a look of haughty cunning,” and decorate their hats with a white feather, the symbol of the mirna [[iolanta|Iolanta]].

In other words, a guard catches someone who has plainly broken the rules, while an alguacil decides whether the rules have been broken. It resembles a division between a police officer and a legal investigator. The word comes from Spanish: *alguacil* means a bailiff or a junior officer of justice.

## An alguacil at work: Diego Ingres (ch. 13)
The tiny station on Foucault Street is on white land, with two men on duty: a guard and an alguacil. When [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] comes to report a threat, it is the alguacil, [[diego-ingres|Diego Ingres]], who goes to inspect the scene, while the guard stays behind to clean his nails. Ingres says that serving as an alguacil on white land in the Arts district means “working with your head and nothing else”: there are no murders or thefts there, but creative people produce cases in which it is unclear whether any crime has occurred.

His analysis shows how an alguacil thinks. The law punishes only an act that contains the elements of an offense. A verbal threat is protected by freedom of speech, any voluntary transaction is legal, and only an injured party may bring a claim. Therefore, in Ingres’s view, the note saying “Stop it!” and the severed finger in Alvaro’s desk do not constitute a crime.

## Where they are absent and where they are needed
- **White land.** Chapter 5 says that on white land “you will encounter neither alguacils nor guards—they simply have nothing to do here.” The station on Foucault Street nevertheless stands in a white neighborhood, and Ingres has little work: he admits himself that ordinary crimes do not occur there ([[belaya-zemlya]]).
- **Isabel’s disappearance (ch. 18).** When [[karlos|Carlos el Niña’s]] wife vanishes near the Eliste Gallery, he gets “both guards and alguacils” scrambling, but they find no trace.
- **The Hall of the Scroll (ch. 22).** In the archives of the [[svitok|Great Scroll]], Alvaro calls on a passing alguacil for help—the narrator calls him a “servant of the Scroll”—and the man shows him how to use the reading machine. Evidently, alguacils also serve at the Scroll itself.
- **Last Winter (ch. 34).** [[fatima|Fatima Ir-Reya]], prim-allen of Law, arrives at the castle with ten armed alguacils and five vehicles; they take away the wounded agent, the bodies, and the surviving killer, who has been tied up ([[posledniaya-zima-shturm]]).
