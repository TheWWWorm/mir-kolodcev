Catalian territory is divided into three kinds of land according to the crimes tolerated there. This division is connected with the [[teni|esfera of Shadow]] and established by tradition: criminals live openly in Catalia, but they follow rules.

## White land (ch. 5–24)
White land is a district where there is no crime at all. White pillars stand at every intersection, and a Catalian accustomed to seeing them feels safe. Here there are no bars on windows or locks on doors, no need for guards or alguacils; children play unsupervised, goods lie unprotected, and young women walk around in provocative dresses ([[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] notices all this in ch. 5, almost forgetting where he is). Holding someone against their will on white land is a crime, which enables Alvaro to leave Maduro’s guardroom with a revolver in his holster (ch. 13). The neighborhood where Alvaro lives, on Foucault Street, is also white, so his home has no lock.

The chief taboo of white land is murder. Killing on white land is criminal in a profound, almost sacred sense: the killer is effectively spitting in the [[pervyj-sudya|First Judge’s]] face, and the punishment is monstrous (ch. 9). The First Judge’s laws take intent into account: if a death is caused unwittingly and without intent to kill, the taboo does not apply, but the court may still impose an extremely severe sentence (ch. 24). For a Catalian, as the novel says, murder on white land is impossible on moral grounds; a foreigner raised differently is physically capable of it, but that very fact also makes them the first suspect (ch. 18, 22).

## Grey land (ch. 5, 11, 24, 34)
Grey land is an intermediate zone. Here, stealing, fraud, and fistfights are permitted, but killing, rape, and maiming are not. The [[tri-byka|Three Bulls]] tavern stands on grey land, at an intersection where the districts of Craft, Service, and Agriculture meet (ch. 5). During the strike, the workers are certain that the factory owners’ guards will not fire: “This is grey land—shooting is forbidden!” The cannons do indeed fire blanks: the factory owners “did not dare fire cannons at their own people, and on grey land at that” (ch. 11).

Murder on grey land is not a sacred taboo as it is on white land, but an ordinary crime for which one is tried. In ch. 24, a junior lieutenant killed in a pistol duel is dissected in the anatomy theater. The doctor explains: “The duel took place on grey land, not black, so the victor stood trial for murder.” In ch. 34, Alvaro reproaches [[salvador|Salvador]] for having “killed two people on grey land.”

## Black land (ch. 7, 20)
In black districts, anything is permissible within the bounds of Shadow’s trades. A classic black district is the Old Port ([[staryj-port]]): a person wearing a pirate’s or assassin’s bracelet may kill there with impunity (ch. 7). The authority of Judge [[sudya-enkhal|Enhal]], Beatriz’s father, ceases to apply on black land (ch. 20).

## White land in the story (ch. 5–24)
The mysterious death of Dr. [[khuanes|Juanes]] in a white district is the central mystery of the novel’s first half. To those around him, murder is “absurd and stupid,” as the butler Emil Diale puts it (ch. 5), and Alvaro rejects the possibility of poisoning for a long time. He proposes theories about a Dangarian spy who would not fear the taboo (ch. 22), and asks Carlos how someone could kill on white land (ch. 18). Carlos’s answer is that blood is not spilled on white sand; a lone madman would not have covered his tracks. In ch. 24, Alvaro explains that Juanes was killed unintentionally: the butler had poured some bonk for himself into an empty vial, and the doctor’s wife mistakenly gave that very vial to her husband; that was why the taboo did not take effect.

## The concept
In interviews, the author did not speak specifically about white, grey, and black land; he merely called Catalia a “triumph of materialism.”
