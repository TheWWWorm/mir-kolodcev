**Iolanta** is the [[mirny|mirna]] of Law. The esfera of Law is called her domain: alguacils, judges, and lawyers are described in the novel as “servants of Iolanta.”

## Iolanta’s symbols (ch. 13–28)
Alguacils wear a white feather in their hats. The narrator calls it one of Iolanta’s symbols (ch. 13). Their pointed beards and mustaches emphasize their haughty, cunning appearance. Alvaro speaks of [[fatima|Fatima]] as “Iolanta’s merciless sword” (ch. 13). The bracelet of the prim-allen of Law is also decorated with a diamond feather (ch. 28).

## How Iolanta became mirna of Law (ch. 15–16)
Red knows Iolanta from his mother’s stories: she is one of the four mirnas his mother liked to tell him about (ch. 15).

In a painting at the “Philosophy of Horror” exhibition, Iolanta and [[alessina|Alessina]] are depicted as two dead girls beside an easel. According to [[filippe|Filippe di Berro]], the traditional story is this: Iolanta persuades Alessina that she understands painting better than Alessina does. Alessina concedes, and Iolanta is recognized as a brilliant debater and made mirna of Law (ch. 16). One of the girls has a feather in her hair; the other wears a theatrical mask.

## Iolanta as an image of the people of Law (ch. 18–28)
- In Alvaro’s drunken vision, Iolanta appears in Alessina’s place “in the form of a drowned woman” and shakes her fist at him, reminding him of morality (ch. 18).
- Alvaro calls Fatima “Iolanta from ‘Philosophy of Horror’” and a “cold, soulless corpse of a woman” when [[mor|Mor]] asks him to influence her (ch. 20).
- The “servants of Iolanta” are contrasted with the workers of the “Three Bulls” in a conversation about the election of the supremo: they are few in number, like the people of Art (ch. 26).
- When Alvaro finds a bracelet with Iolanta’s diamond feather in the unknown woman’s bedroom, it finally convinces him that the woman is [[fatima|Fatima Ir-Reya]], prim-allen of Law (ch. 28). There, he also reflects that the judge’s daughter [[beatris|Beatriz]] could have followed in her father’s footsteps and become a “servant of Iolanta.”

## The concept and interviews
The author described the mirnas as patrons of fields of activity, akin to muses. Among them, Iolanta serves as a muse of law. More details are in [[mirny|“Mirnas”]].
