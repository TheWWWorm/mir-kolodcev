**Mirnas**—*mirn* in the masculine, *mirna* in the feminine—are the patrons of particular pursuits and forces in the novel’s world. Catalians invoke them in oaths and everyday sayings, ask them for inspiration or luck, decorate buildings with their images, and paint them on walls. Most of the mirnas named in the book are women, but there are also men: [[darredzho|Darreggio]] and Flamberto.

## Who the mirnas are
The novel offers several explanations, which do not entirely agree. This is precisely the mythological uncertainty the author has spoken about.

As early as the preface (ch. 0), marble statues of mirnas stand on the roof of the [[galereya-eliste|Eliste Gallery]] above Laerta’s evening boulevard. They are subsequently depicted as clock figures and decorations or invoked in oaths, rather than appearing as active characters.

## What the characters say about mirnas (ch. 4–16)
- Explaining to Dangarians who is depicted in his tattoo, Red calls a mirna “a woman who has not died, a helper of the gods” (ch. 4).
- In his childhood, Red’s mother told him parables about mirnas: each had died, then been reborn and begun to serve the [[drevnie|Ancients]]; they fell into trouble but always found a way out. He names four: Eliga, Alessina, Iolanta, and Clarissa (ch. 15).
- Standing before a painting in the Eliste Gallery, [[filippe|Filippe di Berro]] asserts that people become mirnas after death, and not immediately: first they await judgment (ch. 16). In the story behind the same painting, Iolanta became mirna of Law after winning a debate against Alessina.

## Known mirnas (ch. 3–16)
All the mirnas named in the novel are listed below.
- [[eliga|Eliga]] is the mirna of luck, with two faces: one a woman’s, the other a serpent’s. She is mentioned most often.
- [[alessina|Alessina]] is the mirna of art, creativity, and theater; her symbol is a theatrical mask.
- [[iolanta|Iolanta]] is the mirna of Law; her symbol is a white feather.
- [[vinsenya|Vinsenya]] is the mirna of fertility.
- [[darredzho|Darreggio]] is the mirn of mechanics and engineering.
- Clarissa is the mirna of vitality, or life force. Fernan prays to her when he fears for his life in the pirates’ den (ch. 7).
- Flamberto is the mirn of pleasure. Alvaro jokes that even he would not refuse Juanes’s expensive bonk (ch. 5).
- The mirna of truth is mentioned without a name: the narrator observes that, when mixing fact and fiction in the book about Carlos el Niña, one must take care not to offend her too much (ch. 7).

The novel gives no other names. Clarissa and Flamberto do not have separate articles.

## Mirnas in daily life and the city
- **Clocks.** Figurines of mirnas move around the balcony encircling the tower of the [[akademiya-iskusstv|Academy of Arts]]. Each emerges beneath an enameled sun at her or his appointed hour: this is how the time is told. At two in the afternoon it is [[vinsenya|Vinsenya]], replaced at three by [[darredzho|Darreggio]] (ch. 3, 5).
- **Oaths.** “I swear by Alessina,” “glory to the mirnas,” and “the mirnas are my witnesses” are common expressions used by Alvaro and his friends (ch. 7, 22, 24). Red appeals “by all the Ancients and all the mirnas” even to the Dangarian hunter (ch. 4).
- **Symbols of the esferas.** The white feather in alguacils’ hats is the sign of Iolanta, mirna of Law (ch. 13). Iolanta’s diamond feather adorns the bracelet of the highest rank of the esfera of Law (ch. 28).
- **Temples and churches.** Laerta has a church of Vinsenya (ch. 11) and a grey-stone temple of Darreggio in the middle of Craftsmen’s Square (ch. 30). Alessina Bay on Laerta’s waterfront is the dons’ grand harbor, with yachts and clippers (ch. 7).
- **Painting.** In the workers’ district, walls are daubed with crude, cheerful pictures: a bull with a bullfighter, drunken mirnas, and a sleeping Vinsenya (ch. 13). At the Eliste Gallery, the “Philosophy of Horror” exhibition depicts the mirnas and the Ancients in a dark and shocking form, such as Iolanta and Alessina as two dead girls (ch. 16, 18).

## The concept and interviews
According to the author in the first stream, mirnas are patrons or muses of every field of activity, not just art: engineering and agriculture have their own. They may be male or female, and people turn to them for inspiration. Their role in the characters’ lives is roughly comparable to that of muses in our culture.

The author emphasizes that mirnas are not gods: there is no proof that they really existed, and they are not worshipped. He calls them “muses or angels,” lower in standing than the Foremothers of his other series, and allows that they may be pure myth. They should not be confused with the Foremothers of “Polari.”
