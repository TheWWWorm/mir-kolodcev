The novel portrays the Ollandians mainly through their conquerors’ eyes, so there is little material about their daily life; information about the country, war, and occupation is collected in [[ollandiya|Ollandia]]. This article covers what the text and interviews say about their customs and character.

## Appearance and character
The narrator in ch. 4 describes the country as a land of princely palaces in landscaped gardens, cathedrals with stained glass, colorful hussars in plumed helmets, orchestras, and haughty, rosy-cheeked ladies; pride in the homeland is written on every face, from the stationmaster to the woman selling pretzels. It is also said that if the Ollandians had factories, they would manufacture arrogance. The princes are “full of airs,” constantly competing with one another, and their forces are fragmented (ch. 17).

## Princes and voivodeships
The country is divided into voivodeships. The leader of a voivodeship’s nobility—for example, Prince [[lents|Wladyslaw Lenz]] of Latsen—is the district’s leading figure; his family is called “most ancient,” and he himself has fought since the age of fourteen. Chapter 31 names three voivodeships: Plyutva, Latsen, and Mershants. Even before the war, Prince Lenz understood the weakness of the divided country and tried to persuade the nobles to unite their retinues (ch. 17).

## Estates and slavery (ch. 10)
According to the author, Ollandia has a nobility, bourgeoisie, and peasantry, but no slavery. Dangar fits the conquered into its own system: after the capture of Plyutva, ten thousand of its inhabitants are branded as stones (ch. 10).

## Names
Ollandian names are Slavic: [[olkha|Olkha]], [[lents|Wladyslaw]]. The author said that he chooses names so that they sound unlike one another: Wladyslaw is easy to distinguish from the Dangarian nobles. For Dangarians, Olkha is “Helga in your language” (ch. 6).

## Views of the occupation (ch. 29)
Olkha calls Dietrich “Mr. Occupier” and asks whether he is ashamed to kill (ch. 6); in Hofstadt, Lenz seeks the post of the kaiser’s governor (ch. 25, 29). The author compares Ollandia’s fate with that of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth: “in places, yes, there is a resemblance,” and acknowledges its sad fate. See [[prototipy|Models and Influences]].
