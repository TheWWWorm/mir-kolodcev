Wells are the central mystery of the world in which the novel takes place. These are not ordinary water wells: in a true Well, instead of a bottom there is mist and another world inhabited by monsters. Dangarians call such a well a “schönbrun,” or “Beautiful Well,” and expect a miracle from it; the guild’s fishermen know how to “angle” in it, while Catalians regard wells as curiosities. Following the author’s working name, the entire series about this world is called the “World of Wells.” The novel’s three storylines—those of the Catalian [[red|Red]], the Dangarian [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort]], and the writer [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]]—converge around wells of different kinds.

This article collects the rules governing wells that are stated directly or indirectly in the book. For a detailed discussion of wells of misfortune, see [[temnyj-shyonbrun]]; for the method of digging, see [[tsushtand]].

## What is known from the beginning
For a Dangarian, a well is a place where one awaits a miracle. This is explained by the sacred parable of the [[seyatel|Sower]]: the field yielded no harvest until the Sower dug a well in which “faith in a miracle splashed”; watered by that faith, the wheat finally came into ear. From this comes the Dangarian belief that even the finest words bear no fruit without faith in a miracle, and that faith lives specifically in Beautiful Wells.

Looking into such a well, the first thing one sees is mist, and beyond the mist, hideous monsters. Every educated Dangarian knows this; in conversation with the Ollandian Olkha, Lieutenant von Dort observes that miracles are rarely seen in life, yet the Well is precisely where one must look for them.

Few people own wells. Fishermen hold that beggars do not own wells, while fortunate landowners love their wells for one reason, the slave “stones” for another, and the Fishermen’s Guild for a third. All known schönbruns are marked on the guild’s maps, together with the names of the landowners.

For Catalians, wells are not a matter of faith. The author confirmed in an interview that Catalian society is materialistic and that most Catalians regard wells as amusing curiosities, whereas in Dangar wells and monotheistic religion form the twin foundations of philosophy and mysticism.

## The first wells in the novel (ch. 2–7)
In ch. 2, Lieutenant [[ditrikh|Dietrich]] leads his battery onto a hill with a half-ruined barn on its slope. Deep inside is a well without a railing, simply a hole in the floor with a winch, beside which the simple-hearted soldier [[spasenie|Salvation]] is busy. Dietrich looks into the well before the battle, and it is after this that he wins an engagement which, by every calculation, he should have lost ([[tri-zalpa|The Battle at the Well on the Hill]]). Later, Major [[klyajn|Klein]] investigates this “strange victory,” and Dietrich cannot offer the chief argument in his defense: no one but him saw the well.

In ch. 4, the reader learns that Catalian slaves at a Dangarian “mushroom patch”—a woodland settlement of dugouts—are digging wells. The master fisherman [[joriksen|Joriksen]] explains to Red through gestures that if he digs a “schönbrun,” he will receive freedom, money, clothes, and a train ticket. At first Red thinks that he has misunderstood the translation, then that the promise concerned an ordinary well.

In ch. 7, another well stands in the cellar of Carlos el Niña’s fort. There are two there: an ordinary one and a special one, which a commandant ordered filled in a hundred years earlier after seeing “something terrible” inside it. Carlos and his wife have dug it out again, although they do not know how to use it, and Dangarian fishermen have not seen the well. When a servant removes the cover, white mist fills the shaft, and through it another world is visible: a clearing where a gigantic, withering chrysanthemum grows, strangled by a grapevine. The guests feel bitter anguish, and Doña [[izabel|Isabel]] can barely hold back her tears. After leaving the cellar, [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] quickly forgets what he saw. The book does not say exactly what kind of well it is, light or dark; later, Isabel will name the same image—a vine strangling a flower.

## Schönbrun and brunnen (ch. 15, 27)
Dangarians distinguish between a “brunnen,” an ordinary hole containing water, and a “schönbrun,” a well in which one can “angle.” [[joriksen|Joriksen]] explains this distinction to [[red|Red]] when it emerges that he has not dug the right thing (ch. 15). Water in the hole proves nothing in itself. A true schönbrun is recognized by a different sign: mist at its bottom, and another world beneath the mist (ch. 27).

## Digging a well (ch. 4–19)
In Dangar, wells are dug by slave “stones.” The rules and purpose of digging are described in [[tsushtand]]; what matters here is what they imply for everyone who digs.

- A well is dug by hand: the shaft is measured in yards, earth is raised in buckets on a cable over a pulley, and a rope ladder provides access (ch. 12). Red reaches water in the thirteenth yard.
- The appearance of water means nothing in itself: there have been a great many “failed schönbruns”; most attempts produce an ordinary well, and the settlements have long had water (ch. 15).
- The guild keeps records: a scribe notes the depth and the number of songs and coughing fits Red has (ch. 15, 19). Hundreds of slaves dig simultaneously by different methods—with bare hands, a spoon, or a shard of bottle glass (ch. 19).
- Digging must follow conditions tailored to the digger’s “essence.” Joriksen believes that the Sower dug his well according to his own essence, and that a human can therefore dig one too if the conditions are recreated.
- In the church fresco at Dunkwald, the Sower digs a schönbrun alone with a shovel, and a “golden radiance from the pit” shows that he has dug the well successfully (ch. 19).

In ch. 15–19, Red believes that he has reached his goal: the conditions of “night, a campfire, songs in Catalian, women, and no brand” combine into his personal recipe; water appears, and the cough that has tormented him disappears. The well’s subsequent fate is another story; see “Täuschung” below.

## Fishing (ch. 14, 21)
“Fishing” is the ritual contemplation of a well, led by a fischmeister ([[fishmajster]]). The most detailed example is the fishing session at Baroness [[elza|Elsa von Walbrecht’s]] in Hofstadt ([[rybalka-valbrekht]]). Her well stands on the bank of the River Wunde, and the mansion is styled like a steamship. Fishing takes place there once a year, on the last day of the holy month; that year’s theme is ailments of the heart, and despite the “select company,” tickets are sold for two hundred marks (ch. 10, 14). The schönbrun stands in a circular pavilion with stained-glass windows; inside is a small theater whose ceiling consists of mirrors set at different angles so that every spectator looks down into the well through their own mirror.

The procedure in this scene is as follows: guests write the “heartache” they wish to lose on a slip of paper; using “nautical reckoning,” the master checks the date, time, weather, and number of people aboard—it turns out to be thirty-six rather than thirty-five, with an “extra” person found; the slips are burned with powder, a potion, a bone, flint, and fire steel; the cover is removed. Inside, clear water appears instead of mist, revealing a bottom with a red-and-gold meadow and enormous crabs tearing the earth apart; but the cracks and ravines swallow the monsters, and the meadow heals over. The master declares the fishing successful. Euphoria then sweeps over the guests.

At the same fishing session, Lieutenant [[ditrikh|Dietrich]] tells Joriksen about his “spontaneous fishing” in the barn on the hill. In that well he saw two monsters: a faceless man with a mouth on his belly and rotting feet, and a swarm of little creatures that gnawed at him and were poisoned; the faceless one spat out scraps, and the whole ground became covered in poison. As Dietrich watched, revulsion made him sick, but afterward came a reckless courage—he felt he could “wade through the sea”—that lasted until the effect wore off. Joriksen and the baroness believe him (ch. 14).

In ch. 21, Joriksen explains how his art works. Every schönbrun has its own temperament: one likes spiders, another hates redheads, and the baroness’s schönbrun likes prime numbers. There are no formulas; intuition and experience are necessary. Getting to know a documented well takes one to five days, while a new one takes months. The master’s rate is 500 silver marks per day of familiarization, and the same for a day of fishing.

## Light and dark wells (ch. 21–25)
Joriksen tells Dietrich plainly that there are no cursed wells, but there are light and dark ones. Light wells give “good fish”; dark ones are dangerous. For details about dark wells, see [[temnyj-shyonbrun]]; the main points here are:

- Fish bite rarely; while they are not biting, even a dark well is safe, but this cannot be predicted in advance (ch. 25, according to [[otto|Otto]]).
- A dark well harms the body, reason, memory, feelings, and prosperity, but cannot kill directly: it only helps someone die indirectly.
- The creatures visible in the mist live in another space, “on the other side of the window.” Otto says that criminal stones have been lowered into a dark well and left untouched.
- One must not look into an unknown dark schönbrun without an instrument: the rule says “water cleanses water,” and fishermen use a silver-coated glass flask in which water forms a kind of mirror (ch. 31).
- According to the magnus, the monsters should not escape from the well; a dark schönbrun can bring sickness, bad luck, poverty, despair, and fear (ch. 29).

## The rules of the oberfischmeisters (ch. 23)
Only a few masters of the highest rank can do more than look. According to [[landau|Baron Landau]], a fischmeister can fish, while an oberfischmeister can “take the catch with him”: he imposes a schönbrun’s effects on people—the lieutenant’s eyelids stick together and his arm turns rigid, while the hunter [[yakka|Yakka]] cannot close his mouth. In ch. 33, Landau wonders whether it would be permissible to use this power to turn a chained prisoner into a puppet for a minute and force him to carry out an execution.

## Täuschung (ch. 27)
The behavior of Red’s well becomes clear in ch. 27, where Klein tells the captive Catalian about “Täuschung”—deception. A real schönbrun shows mist at the bottom and another world with monsters. Red’s, however, contained clear water, which means that he dug an ordinary well. On seeing that Red’s cough had disappeared, Baron Landau hoped for a “light spontaneous schönbrun,” but as early as ch. 23 he was whispering over the pit that it was a deception. Red’s recovery is explained without a miracle: he never had a sarcoma; a Laertan doctor mistook bronchitis for one. The history of Dunkwald, Fischmeister Joriksen, and the “conspiracy” surrounding the well is set out in detail in [[joriksen]] and [[dunkvald]]. The guild spent half a century trying to create a well, but not one well dug by mortals became a schönbrun (ch. 27, 31).

## Dangar’s wells in war and politics (ch. 29–31)
By ch. 29, the guild discovers that there is not a single schönbrun in Dunkwald: “the Catalian did not even dig down to water,” as Magnus [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]] puts it. In ch. 31, Major Klein tries to exploit Dietrich’s discovery: he seeks a “schönbrun of victory,” a well that brings success in battle, for General [[khofmajer|Hofmeyer]], who is preparing an offensive toward the Catalian border. Joriksen objects that no conclusions can be drawn from one battle and that an unknown dark well is dangerous—“looking for a coin with a candle in a powder magazine”—but obeys. At this point, the rotting corpse of a man who has been shot lies in the barn’s well.

The barn stands on the land of Prince [[lents|Wladyslaw Lenz]], near the hundredth verst of the Plyutva–Latsen line. According to Joriksen, schönbruns close together “sense one another,” so a well can be found by fishing in a neighboring one (ch. 29). In ch. 31, Klein shows the well to Joriksen and [[otto|Otto]]; Otto looks into it through a silver flask and sees the rotting corpse. Klein believes that Dietrich received precisely the effect of a “schönbrun of victory,” but this is only the major’s hypothesis: the novel does not specify what effect Dietrich experienced. Dietrich negotiated this land as part of the dowry in the document recording his engagement to [[olkha|Olkha]].

## Van Halm’s well (ch. 24, 28, 32, 34)
In the Catalian storyline, wells appear as the cause of mysterious misfortunes. The Merganan philosopher [[liess-van-khalm|Liess van Halm]] settled in a “cursed” castle above the small town of [[tres-molinos|Tres Molinos]]. In ch. 24, [[beatris|Beatriz]] reports that he claimed to have seen all five heroes of Solanta in a well. The investigation leads to Last Winter castle ([[posledniaya-zima]]), where a dark schönbrun is discovered in the cellar beneath the knights’ hall: a black hole in the floor behind a railing of bronze skulls and bones, with dozens of mirrors on the ceiling. In ch. 34, [[fatima|Fatima]] lists three laws of the well of misfortune (more details in [[temnyj-shyonbrun]]): anyone who looks into the water at a particular time, including through a reflection, becomes a victim; misfortune comes by chance; and bad luck strikes what is dearest. According to her, van Halm looked into the well deliberately, wanting to peer beyond the world’s edge and study the world of chaos.

## What remains unclear (ch. 7–34)
- The novel does not explain where schönbruns come from or who created them. Red knows a legend that the Ancients created the wells, while Joriksen objects that the Sower made them.
- The book does not explain why a well shows these particular images—a crab above a meadow, a chrysanthemum and a vine; the fischmeisters merely observe that every well has its own temperament.
- Carlos el Niña’s well remains unclassified: the book makes no direct statement that it is dark.

## Wells in the author’s conception
According to the author, wells are a source of miracles and magic for Dangarians, one of the two foundations of Dangarian philosophy, the other being monotheistic religion. Catalia, by contrast, is what he calls a “triumph of materialism.” The author uses “World of Wells” as a working name for the new series; “Traces on the Water” is its first book (interview iv1). See [[roman-surzhikov]], [[mir-kolodtsev]].
