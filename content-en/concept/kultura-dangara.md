The empire’s organization, army, and history are discussed in [[dangar|Dangar]]; this article collects details of Dangarian daily life: how people address one another, what they believe, how they spend their evenings, and what they think of outsiders. In a stream, the author called Dangar an “embodiment of the ideas of the state,” with a German style of ranks, dress, and kaiser, and stressed that the country’s philosophy contains more profound ideas than may first appear (see [[slovo-avtora|The Author’s Words]]). To a Dangarian, rank and honor mean more than profit, and faith is not an ornament but a load-bearing structure of society.

## Rank, honor, and forms of address
- **Eagles**, or nobles, serve out of a duty of honor. In their circles, resignation means disgrace, and a poor eagle without land or stones can only be an officer (ch. 6). Lieutenant Dietrich’s fellow officers and subordinates call him “Herr Lieutenant,” and a superior officer “Herr Staff Captain” or “Herr Major.” A count is addressed as “Your Grace” or “Your Excellency,” and the kaiser as “His Majesty.” When meeting, people traditionally wish one another “a steady hand” (ch. 2, 14). For more about the estate, see [[orly|Eagles and Hares]].
- **Hares** are free commoners: merchants, scribes, and managers. They enter government service only on a noble’s recommendation.
- **Stones** are slaves. They have no surnames; slaves receive nicknames based on distinguishing features or events—[[siplyj|Hoarse]], [[spasenie|Salvation]], [[boroda-lopatoj|Spadebeard]]. A slave wears a small stone around the neck and a brand on the body. Slaves address officers as “Herr Lieutenant” (ch. 2). See [[kamni|Stones]].
- **Ancestry** is measured in generations: some of the Kaiser’s Claws have grandfathers who were still “hares.” A family name with the particle “von”—[[ejring|Eyring]], Lurd, Walbrecht, Dort—serves as a recommendation: hearing the name Eyring, Prince [[lents|Lenz]] asks in ch. 17 whether Dietrich is one of “those very Eyrings.”

## Faith and the church (ch. 8, 14)
There is a church in every small town, and sometimes even in the wilderness; a silver bird spreads its wings above the entrance. The narrator describes Dunkwald’s church through [[red|Red’s]] eyes: a round hall, old yellowed icons, and, in a niche, a smooth white altar stone resembling a sarcophagus, with dark streaks down its sides. Above the altar is the principal icon: a barefoot man in peasant clothes sows wheat, with eagles soaring above him, hares rubbing against his feet, and stones lying along the sides of the field. The other icons depict suffering and sacrifice: a stag feeds human children with its flesh, a sheep removes its skin to warm a naked girl, and a man crawls, dragging a millstone around his neck.

During the service, the slaves stand in the center of the hall beneath the light from the windows, while the free people crowd along the walls, having yielded the place of honor to the slaves. The priest in white vestments wears a broad gold bracer on his forearm; his assistants in grey cassocks are also stones, with a burned brand in place of an ornament. The slaves make sacred signs and kiss the stones around their necks; choral singing alternates with prayer, and even Red, who is far from devout, admits that it contains much “bright feeling.”

**Feeding.** At the end of the service, the priest scoops up porridge with his bare hand and feeds his assistants with the words “Sorge um dich,” after which the free people feed the slaves: officers, scribes, sergeants, and the master fisherman. The stones stretch their necks and open their mouths “like chicks before a duck.” The ritual is considered virtuous; according to tradition, the stones won it by force of arms in the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. The author confirmed that the stones won the privilege themselves, but deferred the details to later books. See [[seyatel|Sower]].

**The month of gehai.** In Hofstadt, the Wunde dries up once a year, and this month is called *gehai*, “sacred”: the Sower’s tears fill the river, so when it dries up, God is happy. During this time, even a public bath becomes a luxury (ch. 10).

## Wells, fishing, and the new nobility (ch. 14–17)
Faith in miracles is linked with wells: according to the parable, the field bloomed only when the Sower dug a well in which faith in a miracle splashed. A [[kolodets|schönbrun]] is therefore sacred to a Dangarian, while fishing in it is both an amusement and a sacrament. In ch. 17, Count [[lurd|Lurd]] explains the balance of power to Dietrich: the wealthy “new nobility” owns factories making cannons and uniforms and likes to buy miracles; the sellers of miracles, the [[fishmajster|fischmeisters]], become welcome guests in noble homes. The society fishing session at Baroness [[elza|Walbrecht’s]] (ch. 14) is an expensive occasion: a ticket costs two hundred marks, the seating is designed for thirty-five spectators, though thirty-six arrive, and Master Joriksen counts the guests himself. More details: [[rybalka-valbrekht|Fishing at Baroness Walbrecht’s]], [[gildiya-rybakov|Fishermen’s Guild]].

## The kaiser, the court, and the Claws (ch. 17, 21)
The kaiser does not appear in the novel; he is spoken of as unreachable. Count Lurd names three centers of influence in the empire: the kaiser with his army; the patriarch, who through his priests exerts “no less” influence over the minds of the stones; and the magnus of the fischmeisters’ guild. The author left court intrigues for the second book. Around the kaiser are his intimates—for example, Count [[varkhajt|Wahrheit]], described in ch. 14 as His Majesty’s companion. The [[kogti-kajzera|Kaiser’s Claws]], who answer directly to the kaiser, form a distinct group; their emblem is a patch bearing a silver falcon. The palace in Hofstadt is star-shaped, with ten towers; above the capital fly the three-masted airships of the patriarch and the Life Guards, carrying bells and banners (ch. 10). More details: [[khofshtadt|Hofstadt]], [[kajzer|Kaiser]].

## Newspapers
Count [[varkhajt|Wahrheit]], an intimate of the kaiser, owns four newspapers, including “Glory of the Empire”; at the fishing session, Elsa says that his newspapers keep them informed of every piece of news in the world (ch. 14). Dietrich and his landlady learn of the baroness’s fishing session from a newspaper article (ch. 10, 14), and “Glory” reports the empire’s victories and troubles. The Catalian “[[vestnik|Herald]]” is covered separately.

## Hofstadt society (ch. 10, 14)
The capital is enormous—two million people—and has everything from expensive theaters to cheap bathhouses. Poor eagles live in Witwen, the Widows’ District: narrow three-story houses, with two floors for the landlady, her children, and her stones, and the third rented out. The owners pride themselves on descending from Empress Augusta’s first widows. Frau [[gizeke|Gieseke]] receives Dietrich and Milosh at a table with tea and schnapps. Carriages in the capital are often “human-powered”: few horses are available.

The festivities at Baroness Walbrecht’s reveal the nobility’s manners: waltzes in the ballroom, pipes and stoss in the drawing room, where Count Wahrheit holds the bank for a thousand marks, flattering toasts, and the custom of feeding the servants while the nobles stand behind the stones. A dragoon cornet who has expressed contempt for Ollandians begins pursuing Princess [[olkha|Olkha]] as soon as he sees her, and forcibly takes her away from a waltz; Dietrich, whom his comrades in ch. 2 describe as a man not given to dueling, smooths over the scandal with a toast and asks the princess to dance himself.

## Outsiders through Dangarian eyes (ch. 2–29)
- **Catalians** are “windbags”: that is what Lurd and Dietrich call them in ch. 17. The count argues that trading with the windbags is profitable, whereas attacking them is unwise. Joriksen prepares to speak to a Catalian with a Dangarian–Catalian dictionary (ch. 4), while Klein studies their language because he believes war will one day begin with Catalia, whose faith and customs are foreign to Dangar (ch. 27).
- **The Muari.** Dietrich points out to a stone that the Muari do not believe in the Sower, and the Dangarians call the Muari deity the Black Maiden (ch. 2).
- **Ollandians.** The Ollandian Olkha calls Dietrich “Mr. Occupier” in ch. 6, while the fishing guests drink “to the prince” and “to our future allies” (ch. 14). The conquered country’s nobility is sorted into reliable and unreliable—*unsicher*—groups; see [[ollandiya|Ollandia]].
- **Foreign slaves.** On arriving in Dunkwald, a Catalian becomes a “Stein” like everyone else; in practice, a foreigner is more valuable than the locals because the guild believes that he does not know it is impossible to dig a schönbrun (ch. 27).

## The concept
The author stressed that Dangarians are not “villains” to him: his sympathies are directed toward people, not the state. For him, Dangar is a system in which everyone is interconnected: the higher estates take care of the stones, the stones produce goods, and “free the stones and everything will fall apart.” See also [[slovo-avtora|The Author’s Words]].
