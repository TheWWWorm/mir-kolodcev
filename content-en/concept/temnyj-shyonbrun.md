A schönbrun is called dark when fishing in it is dangerous to the observer. The term appears in ch. 21, when [[joriksen|Joriksen]] explains to [[ditrikh|Dietrich]] that there are no cursed wells, but there are light and dark ones. By ch. 34, the Catalian storyline shows that a dark well can ruin the lives of an entire group of people without a single visible perpetrator. For the general rules governing wells, see [[kolodets]].

## What Joriksen says (ch. 21)
Dietrich fears that his victory beside the well was a one-off piece of luck and that the well itself may be dark. Joriksen answers cautiously: there are no cursed wells, but light wells give good fish, while fishing in dark ones is dangerous. He does not explain exactly what makes a well dark; when preparing to fish, he requests a lengthy period of familiarization and a substantial fee, because the temperament of a new schönbrun is not known in advance.

## Otto’s explanation (ch. 25)
The apprentice Otto gives the fullest account of dark wells when Dietrich questions him on the way to the station. According to him:

- Fish are caught rarely. Most of the time they are “not biting,” and then the well is safe, but what will happen cannot be predicted.
- A dark well causes harm in different ways: illnesses of body and mind, loss of memory, sexual potency, or feelings—for example, a mother ceases to love her daughter—and financial ruin.
- It cannot kill directly. It can only assist death indirectly, for example through trembling hands before a duel or fear of dogs.
- The strangers visible in the mist live in another space, “on the other side of the window.” These beings did not touch criminal stones who were lowered into a dark well.

## What the magnus and fishermen say (ch. 29, 31)
The guild’s magnus, [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]], reminds them that the monsters should not escape from the well, and that a dark well can inflict sickness, bad luck, poverty, despair, and fear on people. In ch. 31, Otto takes out a glass flask with a silver coating on one side: the water inside forms a liquid mirror. Under the rule that “water cleanses water,” one must not look into an unknown dark well without such a device. Ordered by Major [[klyajn|Klein]] to study the newly found well, Joriksen objects that an unknown dark schönbrun is “like looking for a coin with a candle in a powder magazine.”

## Last Winter castle (ch. 24, 32)
In the Catalian storyline, the dark schönbrun lies beneath [[liess-van-khalm|van Halm’s]] castle above the small town of [[tres-molinos|Tres Molinos]]. The history of the “cursed house” began long before the philosopher arrived. The café owner, Senora [[markes|Marques]], shows visitors her “museum of misfortunes”—stories of the disasters that befell the inhabitants: the hidalgo Ernesto Ermidoro lowered his lance onto his horse and broke his own neck in the lists; a condottiere with a young wife met death through a crossbow bolt and a noose; a bank robber was sealed in by his own explosion; and a certain Nivendey won an enslaved woman, the sultan’s fifth wife, at dice and ended up behind bars. Van Halm himself said that the curse gave him a chance to “look beyond the edge of the world.” In ch. 28, [[beatris|Beatriz]] reports that the philosopher wrote to her mother claiming to have seen the five heroes of Solanta in the well.

## The three laws of the well of misfortune (ch. 34)
In the castle cellar, beneath the knights’ hall, Alvaro and his friends find the well: a black hole in the center of the room, surrounded by a railing of bronze skulls and bones. Dozens of mirrors on the ceiling all face the hole. The castle’s drinking water comes through pipes from a tank and a wind-driven pump, and a pipe leads into the cellar, giving Alvaro the clue to the hiding place.

[[fatima|Fatima]], who has visited Tres Molinos incognito, explains how this schönbrun works:

1. **Anyone who looks into the water at a particular time becomes a victim.** She does not know exactly when, and therefore asks them never to look—neither at the water nor at its reflection in a mirror.
2. **Misfortune comes by chance.** It cannot be guarded against, but it can be taken into account: anything, however strange, may happen to the victim.
3. **Bad luck strikes what is dearest.** If another person is what someone holds dearest, misfortune may take that person away. Fatima therefore asks [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] and Beatriz not to become “too dear” to her.

Fatima derived these rules by taking apart a lock in the castle ([[posledniaya-zima]]) and finding a rusty component: from it she reconstructed the chain of accidents that ended in van Halm’s madness. According to Alvaro’s account (ch. 34), the night was extremely cold, the grease in the lock thickened, and the mechanism jammed, although the door was not actually locked. The philosopher decided that he had been locked in and spent a month without paper or pen; when a thaw came and softened the grease, the door could have been opened with one turn, but he did not try. He lost his mind.

## The trial at the well (ch. 34)
Convinced by Alvaro’s theory about bad luck, the killer Salvador demands that the writer put it to the test by looking into the well himself. In the cellar, Fernan extinguishes the lantern and strikes Salvador in the face with it as the latter tilts his head toward the mirrors; Alvaro fires, and Salvador burns like a living torch. “The well is where the pipes lead,” Alvaro reasons, locating it by the plumbing. In the light of the smoldering clothes, Beatriz points to the black hole surrounded by mirrors: it is a dark schönbrun, and Alvaro tells them not to look into either the pit or the mirrors. When Frey and Fatima enter the cellar, she asks Alvaro not to look into the well “even to spite me.” Beatriz declares that her Esperanza steam car is the best protected against curses.

## Whom the curse touched (ch. 34)
Alvaro’s and Fatima’s words form a picture: the five heroes of Solanta visited the castle—[[khuanes|Juanes]], [[karlos|Carlos el Niña]], [[kristobal-maduro|Maduro]], [[dezerej|Deserey]], and Fatima herself—and each looked into the well. A couple of days later, Carlos received a letter from the living [[red|Rodrigo da Vira]]. What happened next: Juanes died from his own elixir; Carlos lost his wife; and Maduro, who had seen the well, was, according to Fatima, horrified by what happened to the philosopher. Alvaro suspects that disaster on the battlefield hangs over Deserey and that Fatima’s curse involves losing a loved one; these are only his conjectures, with no confirmation in the text. At first, Alvaro thought the guests had been given water from the well to drink, but Salvador corrected him: drinking is not the point; fishing requires looking into the water. Fatima’s law says the same thing: looking is dangerous.

## Themes and questions (ch. 34)
- The well of misfortune behaves like chance rather than malicious intent. In the novel, it is connected with the goddess of chance, [[eliga|Eliga]], whose other side is [[alessina|Alessina]]. Alvaro compares the curse to poison that Eliga holds on the teeth of her serpent’s face.
- It is unclear why the well works at a particular time or what determines that time.
- The book does not say how the dark well in the castle is connected with the “special” well in Carlos’s cellar described in ch. 7.
