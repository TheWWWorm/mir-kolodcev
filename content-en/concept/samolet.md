An **airship** in the world of “Traces on the Water” is not a winged machine, but a genuine flying ship, with a hull, masts, sails, and cannons. The Russian term used here, *samolyot*, literally means “flies by itself.” Its lift comes from the wood of the flying **brante** trees. In the novel, airships are seen only in the sky above [[dangar|Dangar’s]] capital, while [[kataliya|Catalia]] is only beginning to build them. This is one of the few marvels of the World of Wells that no one considers a miracle: in the discussion of Catalian Truth, it serves as an example of an obvious fact.

## Brante wood (ch. 17, 22)
The secret of an airship lies in the wood. In ch. 22, the Catalian concept of Truth is illustrated with this example: “‘Brante wood produces lift as long as it has not dried out.’ This corresponds completely to reality. Anyone fortunate enough to have seen an airship, or at least an aerial circus performance, will confirm every word.” Brante is therefore used in aerial circuses as well as airships. Not everyone has been lucky enough to see them, however: the spectacle is apparently rare.

Brante does not grow everywhere. In ch. 17, Count [[lurd|Ludwig von Lurd]] tells Lieutenant [[ditrikh|Dietrich]] about Catalia’s treaty with [[solanta|Solanta]]: pirates of the esfera of Shadow captured a coastal fort, and the doge allowed Catalians “to purchase flying raw material on Solanta, which he had not permitted before.” Dietrich immediately exclaims: “Brante trees! They grow on Solanta?!” The count replies: “Yes, they have a grove or two there.” The flying trees concern Dietrich more than anything else, but the count dismisses it: “Yes, Catalia will be able to build a few airships, but that is a mere trifle!” His concern is something else: trade is bringing Catalia closer to Muaris. The conversation shows that Catalia has no brante of its own, while Dangar already has airships.

## Airships over Hofstadt (ch. 10)
Returning from the war to [[khofshtadt|Hofstadt]], Dietrich listens to his wife [[mari|Mari’s]] stories. On the first day of gehai, the sacred month when the River Wunde dries up, the patriarch’s airship flew over their house: “Very beautiful—all gold, banners, bells, and a choir. Singing and chiming poured straight out of the sky.” Everyone fell to their knees and prayed, while little [[khajnts|Heinz]] pointed his finger and said “Aburva.” “‘Aburva’ is his word for an airship.”

That same night, Dietrich sees an airship himself. He is sitting outside with [[kamni|stones]] who transport corpses when suddenly “a gigantic black something blotted out the moon.” It was “a three-masted airship, as enormous as a ship of the line.” At night it traveled “without lights or ringing bells, even without sails.” Behind the stern fluttered the Life Guards’ white standard bearing the face of the [[seyatel|Sower]], while “a long row of gunports stood open, exposing the cannon barrels to the moonlight.” The airship glided over the capital and vanished behind the walls of Schlissel. “A wondrous wonder!” someone exclaimed, and Dietrich agreed.

The two scenes make clear who owns Dangar’s airships. One belongs to the Patriarchate and is brought out on a holy day as a flying temple. The other is a Life Guards warship with gunports—in other words, a military fleet in the sky.

## Airships in Catalia (ch. 13)
In Catalia, airships are mentioned in passing, as a remote luxury. Refusing to visit [[kristobal-maduro|Don Maduro]], [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] lists every means of travel: “I will not walk, ride a horse or a steam carriage, fly in an airship, or sail in a ship.” In this list, an airship stands alongside a steam carriage and a ship. Catalians know of it, but judging by von Lurd’s words, they do not yet have a fleet of their own.

## Observations
The following are comparisons with the real world. The author has not commented on them; these are the wiki’s observations.
- **Not an airplane.** The World of Wells’ airship is closest to a dirigible: it is lighter than air and stays aloft through the lifting properties of a material, rather than a wing and engine. Its construction, however, resembles an eighteenth-century sailing ship of the line, with masts and gunports. Such “sky frigates” are a common fantasy image.
- **A strategic raw material.** Brante plays the same role as shipbuilding timber, saltpeter, or helium in real history: a country without it depends on a supplier. That is why the treaty with Solanta interests Dietrich.
- **The patriarch’s airship** resembles a religious procession or the blessing of a city transferred into the sky: bells, banners, and a choir.
- The word *samolyot* existed in Russian long before aviation: it was used for flying carpets and river ferries. In the novel, it returns to the old meaning of “something that flies by itself.”
