“Traces on the Water” opens Roman Surzhikov’s second fantasy world, the [[mir-kolodtsev|World of Wells]]. The author’s previous series, “Polari,” is complete, and none of its names appear in the novel: there is no Erwin, Minerva, Harmon, or even the word “Polari.” If references exist, they are hidden in the writer [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva’s]] conversations about his craft: lectures to students, arguments about books, and retellings of other people’s plots.

The author himself calls Polari and the World of Wells different turns of a single spiral: for him, the spiral is Polari’s sacred symbol, and the worlds exist on its different turns. It is impossible to move physically from one world to the other, so direct crossovers should not be expected. Echoes of shared devices, jokes, and themes are possible, however.

Below are passages in which Alvaro’s remarks or assessments of his books echo Polari or what the author has said about it. The article titles follow the Russian Polari Wiki and are not links within this encyclopedia. A degree of confidence is given for each point: an **explicit reference** is confirmed by the author; **probably** indicates a persuasive resemblance in technique and plot; **possibly** indicates only an echo.

## The preface and the lesson on the inciting incident (ch. 0, 5)
- In the preface, Alvaro argues that a good book begins with exposition, then tells “our” story himself in the third person. In ch. 5, he lectures his students on the inciting incident using a real case: the mysterious death of Dr. [[khuanes|Juanes]], a dead man’s letter, and suspects appearing one after another. **Probably**, this echoes Lady Karen’s remarks in the collector’s edition of “Arrow, Coin, Spark”: she finds the inciting incident of the Coin storyline only in the fifth chapter, mocks this “discovery in literary theory,” and promises to write a novel whose inciting incident appears on the last page (see Polari Wiki: “Author [in the world of Polari],” “Arrow, Coin, Spark [collector’s edition]”).
- Alvaro’s very position—a writer collecting the stories of real protagonists and putting them into a book—repeats the collector’s edition’s device. There too, the novel exists within its own world, its unnamed author becomes a character, and the protagonists discuss whether they are satisfied with their portraits. **Probably**, this is a deliberate game. In a stream, the author confirmed that Alvaro is writing the book “Traces on the Water” itself.
- The poisoned tea and “poison in a drink” in ch. 5, 22, and 24 recall the poisoning of Mira in “Arrow, Coin, Spark,” which she solves by observing someone else’s reaction to a question about drinking (see Polari Wiki: “Mira’s Poisoning,” Minerva). **Possibly**, this is merely a shared detective-story motif, with no explicit connection.

## El Niña wants to be a tiger (ch. 7)
- The pirate Don Carlos asks to be made a true villain: according to him, Alvaro’s books contain two kinds of villains, “invisible rats” and “bold tigers.” Alvaro replies that his book is biographical and has no antagonist. **Possibly**, this is a joke about Polari’s structure, in which the main adversary, the Puppeteer, operates from the shadows (see Polari Wiki: “Puppeteer,” “The Yanmei Hunt”). There is no direct confirmation.
- Fernan’s idea of a machine that writes novels to order is more closely connected with the theme of artificial intelligence in the author’s other projects—the novel about Unlucky Chris and the question of Navi in Polari—than with the series’ plots.

## The general’s demolition of the novel (ch. 9)
- At Ortiyar Fortress, General [[dezerej|Gabriel Deserey]] praises Alvaro’s new novel and then dissects it in detail: the marquis who fancies himself a strategist splits his forces, conducts no reconnaissance, and walks into ambushes; Ilana sends her vassals into a hopeless attack; the enemies are stupider than the heroes; and the less said about the artillery, the better. Alvaro blushes. The marquis who “splits his forces” is **probably** based in part on Erwin Origin, who also likes to divide his army. During the invasion of the Southern Way, he divided his army into three columns, a “trident,” and in Faunterra his mounted Xs scattered through the streets in groups of twenty (see Polari Wiki: Erwin Origin, “Origin’s Rebellion,” “Battle for Faunterra”). There is an **explicit reference** to criticism of military scenes: in a stream, the author said that Deserey was modeled on a military acquaintance who wrote five pages of criticism of Polari—the battle was described incorrectly, the swords were the wrong length—and ended with “very cool.” Similar criticisms of military scenes and weapons occur in Lady Karen’s remarks and in Manson’s comment about the army (see Polari Wiki: “Author [in the world of Polari]”).
- The student [[beatris|Beatriz]] brings up a “quadratic relationship” between artillery effectiveness and the number of shots. The general is delighted, while Alvaro objects that literature should celebrate heroism, not count cannonballs. **Probably**, this echoes arguments over the credibility of battles in Polari; Karen’s favorite mistake is said to be the scene in which Minerva teaches a general the art of war (see Polari Wiki: “Author [in the world of Polari]”; stratems). It is also possible that the calculation “one to ten, and then another million” echoes the way war is discussed in Polari through the game of stratems (see Polari Wiki: “Stratems”).
- Beatriz outlines a future war novel: an ordinary soldier is separated from his beloved, his friends die one after another, and in the end he dies too. The general approves of its “maturity”; Alvaro calls it “squeezing out tears.” **Possibly**, this is an argument about how to write war, rather than a reference to a particular book.

## The critic in the bookshop (ch. 16)
- In Jose Flarbo’s shop, an unknown lady says that her friend loves Alvaro’s novels for “the exploits of women and brilliant men,” and undertakes to “debunk” the heroines’ exploits. Alvaro then analyzes his own books without identifying himself: a fearless captive says “kill me if you like,” although she could not have known her enemies’ political calculations; a detective nicknamed Kite makes deductions the reader cannot verify, and the author then adjusts the clues to fit them.
  - **Probably**, this is the author’s self-mockery about arguments over female characters and “too brilliant” protagonists. In a stream, the author said that he considers his female characters successful and attributes some of the hostility toward Minerva to readers’ sexism.
  - The captive unafraid of death **possibly** refers to Mira in Weymar captivity or to Laura Farway in “I Want a Pony” (see Polari Wiki: Minerva, “I Want a Pony”). The detective Kite, who produces a complete picture of a crime after one examination of the body, **probably** winks at Mark, nicknamed the Crown’s Raven, the Protection detective from Polari: another bird of prey and another investigator (see Polari Wiki: Mark Raven).
  - Alvaro’s example itself **probably** retells a specific scene. Kite examines a body and declares that there were two killers; they came from the south in a dark carriage drawn by two horses, strangled the victim with a garrote, and took a draft of a newspaper. In “To One Star Alone,” Deputy Sheriff Fairfax brings Mark to a strangled body near a railway embankment. From a single examination, Agent Redhead deduces that the garrote is not a robber’s weapon, and Mark orders him to obtain a draft of the latest “Voice of the Crown”: the dead man proves to be connected with the printer who typeset that issue (see Polari Wiki: Redhead, Mark Raven, “Voice of the Crown”). The garrote, the newspaper draft, and the detective whose conclusions come at a glance coincide too closely to be accidental.
- Alvaro says that he reads books “like an engineer,” taking apart their construction. This echoes the way the author himself analyzes plots in his writing courses, as well as the theme of the “author within the world”: Karen also reads the novel with a pencil in hand.

## The dungeon and the barrels (ch. 26)
- In the twenty-sixth chapter, Alvaro takes Fatima into the former royal cellar of the Valentian dynasty, now a restaurant. He tells her how he once came to the owner and said: “I am working out a terrible scene: a young girl is tortured in a castle dungeon, and her friend’s body is stuffed into a barrel.” On the way, he taps a mossy barrel and tells it, “Sleep peacefully.” **Probably**, this is a direct reference to the darkest scene in “Arrow, Coin, Spark.” In Weymar, Martin Sheyland keeps Mira in the castle dungeon, having previously tortured to death her friend Lindsey, who was captured in Mira’s place. He shows Mira Lindsey’s body in a barrel of brine, and barrels containing bodies later haunt Mira in her delirium (see Polari Wiki: Lindsey, Martin Sheyland, Minerva).
- The joke is that the “torture” cellar turns out to be a luxurious hall with frescoes and velvet armchairs, and Alvaro exclaims, “Perfect!” The author is making fun of how frightening scenes are written.

## The antagonist as a narrative role (ch. 22, 24)
- In the practical lesson “The Antagonist,” Alvaro and Beatriz analyze the invisible enemy’s actions point by point. Beatriz observes that an antagonist is a role, not a judgment. In ch. 3, Alvaro adds that the conflict between protagonist and antagonist must be more important than “kill the hero.” **Probably**, this sets out the author’s own views: he has said that he dislikes explaining away a villain through childhood trauma and values charismatic, amoral characters (interview); there is no connection here with a particular Polari villain.
- In ch. 24, Alvaro and Beatriz joke that “by the laws of the genre,” the murderer is the person who cannot be suspected, and an unopened letter must contain the solution. These are ordinary genre jokes, but **possibly** they wink at Polari readers and their love of searching for the Puppeteer. There is no explicit reference.

## Last Winter and First Winter (ch. 34)
- The castle above [[tres-molinos|Tres Molinos]], where [[red|da Vira]] takes revenge on the heroes of Solanta, is called simply the House Above by the locals. [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] gives it the name [[posledniaya-zima|Last Winter]] in the final chapter, which has the same title. **Possibly**, this is an inverted reference to First Winter in Polari, the ancestral castle and capital of the Origins, where, according to legend, the Foremothers spent their first winter (see Polari Wiki: First Winter, House Origin). This is a hypothesis: the author has not mentioned such a connection.
- In favor of the suggestion are the mirrored name used by the same author and the place’s role: in Polari, First Winter is the beginning and stronghold of House Origin, while Last Winter in “Traces on the Water” is a ruined castle where the storylines converge and the novel ends.
- Against it: “last winter” is an ordinary phrase, and the name has its own reason within the plot. The castle’s “cursed” door would not open on a cold night because the grease had thickened. The resemblance may therefore be coincidental.

## Notes beyond Polari (ch. 3, 7, 34)
- In ch. 3, Alvaro teaches a creative-writing course and gives up time for lectures because he needs the money. The author has said that he runs courses at three levels, studied under a mentor, the art therapist Masha Khrapacheva, and wants to write a textbook on plot. This refers to the author’s life, not the series.
- In ch. 34, Beatriz says that “it is boring to read chapters in books that have no girls in them at all.” **Probably**, this is the author’s joke about the fact that “Traces on the Water” has three male protagonists. In a stream, he admitted regretting that he had not made one of the three a woman.
- In ch. 3, a student in the front row offers an “idea for a novel”: a famous heroic father, a son who reads his papers and learns the bitter truth. Alvaro replies that there is no such thing as an idea for a novel. **Possibly**, this echoes the theme of Erwin Origin. The heir, regarded as a failure by his celebrated father, Duke Desmond, both fears his father and seeks his approval (see Polari Wiki: Erwin Origin, Desmond Origin). The resemblance is general, so the conclusion is cautious.

The article does not claim to be exhaustive: in the known interviews, the author named only Deserey, while the other similarities—the marquis and Erwin, the barrels, Kite, Last Winter—were noticed by readers. The rest consists of readers’ comparisons, which a Polari reader may confirm or reject independently.
