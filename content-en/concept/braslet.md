A bracelet is the chief visible indication of a Catalian’s status. It shows the esfera to which a person belongs ([[esfera]]) and their rank within it. A person’s profession can be read from their bracelet; some bracelets, such as those of pirates and assassins, grant the right to do things that would be crimes for anyone else. Someone may wear several bracelets: in ch. 5, the speaker in the tavern has three—a wedding bracelet, a friendship bracelet, and the bracelet of an obrero of Craft.

## What is known about bracelets (ch. 0–22)
- **The silver bracelet of a maestro.** The writer [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]] wears one on his left wrist: he is a maestro of Art. By the age of thirty-five, he hopes to exchange it for a gold one after gaining fame for a masterpiece (ch. 0).
- **The gold bracelet of a don.** A don is the head of an esfera or of a rank within it. A gold bracelet is not inherited: a don’s son receives money and knowledge from his father, but not the title (ch. 7).
- **White gold with a diamond feather.** The bracelet of the prim-allen of Law: the feather is the symbol of the mirna [[iolanta|Iolanta]], patroness of law (ch. 28). Fatima’s lawyers wear bracelets with a black snake (ch. 1).
- **Black and yellow:** the bracelet of an obrero of Craft; ordinary obreros generally wear black (ch. 5). A black bracelet with three gold gears belongs to an engineer of the highest rank in the esfera of Production ([[mor|Mor]], ch. 20).
- **Green:** the bracelet of a hired chauffeur (ch. 11); a prostitute’s bracelet is worn by women of the esfera of Shadow who are permitted to practice that profession (ch. 13).
- **Pirates’ and assassins’ bracelets.** In “black” districts, a person with such a bracelet may kill without punishment (ch. 7). Rodrigo da Vira, who killed a Muari on black land, did not wear an assassin’s bracelet and was therefore tried (ch. 22). [[karlos|Carlos el Niña]] has the highest bracelet of the esfera of Shadow: he obtained it by winning a debate in the Chamber of the Word (ch. 7).
- **Red’s bracelet.** Red tells [[greta|Greta]] that he changed his former trade, spent four years studying from books, and passed the examination in the Chamber of the Word; he wore his new bracelet, silver with stars, for only six months before learning of his illness (ch. 12, 15).

## How bracelets are earned and lost (ch. 1–22)
A bracelet is awarded following a debate: the Chamber of the Word tests the candidate’s knowledge and skills, and a record remains on the Great Scroll. Rodrigo da Vira himself won a debate for the title of master navigator with “unquestionably compelling arguments”—just as Alvaro did for the title of maestro (ch. 22). An esfera bracelet may also be staked in a [[duelnyj-spor|dueling debate]] (ch. 1). Renouncing a bracelet is a gesture of despair: in ch. 13, Alvaro desperately tries to throw his away, and [[malena|Malena]] flings his maestro’s bracelet after him.

## Hidden bracelets (ch. 5–32)
The butler Emil hides his bracelets beneath his sleeve (ch. 5); van Halm’s hired servants, who were Dangarians, wore no bracelets, which the townspeople remembered (ch. 32). A colorful detail: [[joriksen|Joriksen]] keeps a collection of Catalian curiosities, including a porcelain hand wearing bracelets (ch. 21).

## The idea
For Catalians, a bracelet is not just an indication of status, but a reward for skill. The author speaks of the Catalian “dream”: making one’s way from ordinary worker to maestro and don through one’s own labor.
