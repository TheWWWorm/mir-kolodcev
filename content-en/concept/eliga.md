**Eliga** is the [[mirny|mirna]] of luck. In popular belief, she has two faces: one womanly and kind, the other a serpent’s. If she smiles, good luck follows; if she shows her serpent face, disaster strikes. Catalians invoke her in gambling and in any other situation where everything depends on chance.

## Her image (ch. 4)
Red describes Eliga in terms of luck, “odd or even,” heads or tails. A beautiful woman sitting astride a cannon is tattooed on his forearm. Her womanly face is smiling, while the serpent’s face hides beneath a mass of hair, with only an eye and its vertical pupil visible.

## Eliga in paintings and books (ch. 16–28)
In a painting at the “Philosophy of Horror” exhibition, Eliga sits on a gaming table in front of three cardplayers, with a bullet hole in her temple (ch. 16). The right half of her face, mischievous and feminine, faces the players; the left is in shadow. Alvaro notices that there is no actual second face here: the viewer sees a single woman’s face, half hidden in shadow. From this, he concludes that luck has no dark half, and that success and failure are inseparable.

Among the ten or so books in [[fatima|Fatima’s]] bedroom is a work titled “Myths of Eliga: Fate’s Two Grins” (ch. 28).

## Eliga and Red (ch. 4–23)
Red says that he believes in “brains, fists, and the mirna Eliga—in precisely that order.” This formula recurs in ch. 8, when he refuses to pray with the Dangarian slaves, and in ch. 12, when he tells [[greta|Greta]] how he earned his first bracelet. Originally, he said “fists and Eliga,” adding brains after he passed his examination with the best result of the year. In ch. 19, the Ancients are added to the list.

The tattoo functions as both a sign and an argument. [[joriksen|Joriksen]] tests Red by tossing a coin. Red wagers with him on heads and tails; the coin lands on its edge, and the fischmeister calls him “luck” (ch. 4). Later, Baron [[landau|Landau]] sees the tattoo on the dying Red’s arm, and the [[klyajn|Black Falcon]] explains who it depicts (ch. 15). The hunter [[yakka|Yakka]] calls the pictured woman “the two-faced one” and asks her to “translate” his words for Red as if she were a living spirit (ch. 15). When Red makes plans for the future, the narrator observes that Eliga has already worn him out with her whims (ch. 23).

## Eliga in Laerta (ch. 7–18)
- Carlos el Niña plays “roulette” with his guests using a double-barreled pistol. The right barrel proves empty, and the pirate tells Alvaro that Eliga has smiled on him, but not on [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan]] (ch. 7).
- In a conversation with [[salvador|Salvador]], the latter angrily tells Alvaro that “Eliga smiled” on him (ch. 13).
- A drunken [[karlos|Carlos]] reasons that Eliga has measured out a cup of misfortunes for every person. At the top are little things, such as a hundred lost escudos; farther down lie greater troubles (ch. 18). In answer to a question about [[khuanes|Juanes’s]] death, he also says: “Then the two-faced mirna.”

## The Juanes case and van Halm (ch. 24–34)
- After the autopsy, Alvaro explains that Dr. [[khuanes|Juanes’s]] death was a chain of coincidences: Eliga “showed her serpent face” (ch. 24). The story is examined in detail in [[smert-khuanesa|“The Death of Dr. Juanes”]].
- At the clinic, [[liess-van-khalm|van Halm]] cowers in a corner in terror on seeing the words “ELIGA. BAD LUCK” written in chalk (ch. 26). From this, Alvaro concludes that Eliga can kill and ruin someone through chance, but cannot starve them: the philosopher therefore fears her for one reason and went hungry for another.
- In ch. 34, Alvaro connects the curse of [[posledniaya-zima|Last Winter]] castle with Eliga: those who drank from or looked into the dark schönbrun’s water acquire bad luck. Alvaro attributes the shot that wounded the second killer in the cellar to the intervention of Eliga and Alessina. The letter Carlos receives from the living da Vira bears the note “the two-faced one’s bullet.”

## The concept and interviews
The author said that, for Catalians, the mirnas are like muses rather than gods, and are not worshipped. Eliga is the one mentioned most often. For more about the author’s interpretation, see [[mirny|“Mirnas”]].
