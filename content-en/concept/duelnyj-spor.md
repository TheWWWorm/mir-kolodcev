The dueling debate is the central institution of Catalian law. Everyone, according to the First Judge’s code, may defend their rights through debate. Catalians call themselves people of the word: conflicts that other peoples settle with bullets are brought to the Chamber of the Word. Any question—from the position of a fence between fields to the foundations of the state—may be challenged; only what has been proved in a fair debate is considered correct.

## How a debate works (ch. 1, 22)
Under the First Judge’s code, a debate requires:
- an **arbiter** with a bracelet of the esfera of Law, who takes an oath with a hand on the code;
- **duelists** or their substitutes;
- at least five **jurors**. They must either know neither of the two debaters or know both equally well. Jurors are unpaid and attend for the spectacle: the Chamber of the Word is called a free theater.

The procedure is as follows: the secretary records the opponent’s name; the bailiff summons the jurors; a small weather vane determines who speaks first. The winner is the person whose arguments persuade the jurors. The aim is to convince the jurors, not to change the opponent’s mind; victory is recorded on the Scroll and becomes truth until challenged in a new debate.

The stake may be money, an esfera bracelet ([[braslet]]), or, in tragic cases, a human life. There are comic disputes over trifles as well as dramas involving divorce or the division of an inheritance. Debates also establish ranks: Rodrigo da Vira won a debate for the title of master navigator, and Alvaro for the title of maestro (ch. 22). Arbiters are on duty in the Chamber of the Word all day.

## Alvaro’s debate with Ramires (ch. 1–3)
In the first chapter, [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro]] is dining with his obrero friends, [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan]] and [[lyusil|Lucille]], in an expensive restaurant when a stranger wearing the bracelet of a master of Law reproaches him for bringing “two-sevenths of a person,” invoking the principle that “one equals seven.” Alvaro challenges him to a revolver duel, but his opponent, [[vinsente-ramires|Vincente Ramires da Rimini]], reminds him that true-born Catalians are people of the word and proposes a debate. Confident in his eloquence, Alvaro accepts; the stake is a month’s income.

The next day, in the Chamber of the Word, an arbiter who knows them both takes the oath. Alvaro speaks first and eloquently describes the previous evening, without having prepared. Unexpectedly, Ramires agrees with his facts and argues the central point: the statement “one equals seven” is true because people are unequal. Alvaro appeals to reason, feelings, descent from the Ancients, and love; Ramires produces newspaper reviews and proves that the writer is loved more than anyone else in the room, which means people are unequal in that respect too. He then springs a second successful trap: “monthly income” is ruled to mean income in the current month—seven thousand escudos, the fee for the book. The arbiter tells Alvaro that he must not allow a debate to descend into truisms, and expresses sympathy: Alvaro was doomed from his very first argument. He has three days to pay. Details are in [[duelnyj-spor-ramiresa]].

## Other debates in the novel (ch. 7–24)
- Obreros ([[obrero]]) challenge the factory owners’ right to dismiss workers without cause; the factory owners put forward legal professionals from Law and win (ch. 7, 11). Later, the factory owners defeat bankers who challenge the ban on preferential loans (ch. 20).
- The title of don must be “earned and proved in a disputation” (ch. 7). Carlos el Niña obtained the highest bracelet of the esfera of Shadow in precisely this way.
- A duel with weapons does not protect anyone from prosecution: in ch. 24, officers duel with pistols on grey land, and the winner is tried for murder ([[belaya-zemlya]]).

## The concept and interviews
In an interview, the author explained that the First Judge founded modern Catalia and the system of dueling debates, and created the Great Scroll. He considers the First Judge’s influence on Catalians greater than that of the ancient gods, and promised to explain more about it in the future.
