The Russian original of [[sledy-na-vode|Traces on the Water]] contains not a single paragraph in an invented language: the entire text is written in Russian, and foreign speech is represented in three ways. First, the characters are simply “translated” into Russian. Second, words and phrases drawn from real-world languages according to a particular model are mixed into the text. Third, the text shows non-native speakers breaking Russian grammar. In the spring stream, the author explicitly identified the models: **Catalian is Spanish, and Dangarian is German**; he “had not thought about” Ollandian because he never needed to write anything in it (see [[slovo-avtora|The Author's Words]]). The rest of this article is inferred from the text.

## Catalian
The language of [[kataliya|Catalia]] and the native language of most characters in the “Catalian” storyline: [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]], [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan Gereira]], [[karlos|Carlos el Niña]], [[red|Red]], and others. Its sound is not represented in the text: the narrator uses Russian, but names, ranks, and place names follow Spanish models. The author says its style and naming system are “copied” from Spain.

**Forms of address and titles.** Señor, señorita, doña—a married lady, for example Doña [[izabel|Isabel]]—don, and maestro. Speakers in the [[tri-byka|Three Bulls]] tavern address their listeners as “amigos” (ch. 5).

**Terms for the social structure.**
- **Esfera**—a professional estate; see [[esfera|Esferas]].
- **Allen**—an esfera's representative at the [[vysokij-stol|High Table]]; see [[alleny|Allens]]; **prim-allen**—the first among them.
- **Supremo**—the elected head of state.
- **Obrero**—a worker (rendered as “arera” in the stream transcript); see [[obrero|Obreros]].
- **Alguacil**—an officer of the law from the esfera of Law; in Spanish, the same word denoted a judicial enforcement officer.
- **Hidalgo**—a knight in the opera Señorita Violet (ch. 3); the opera's protagonist is named Don Laurent.
- **Escudos, pesetas, pesos**—money; see [[dengi|Money]].
- **Mirn, mirna**—a male or female patron; see [[mirny|Mirnas]].
- **Sinkho**—a mourning tree; see [[sinkho|Sinkho]].

**Food and drink.** Paella (ch. 1, 13), Alan wine (ch. 1), bonk, including Almagarian bonk (ch. 4–5), and cofenado, a bottled drink Lucille brings in lieu of an apology (ch. 9). The novel does not explicitly explain what cofenado and bonk are: bonk is a strong drink that can quiet a cough, while cofenado is a dark liquid in a small bottle.

**Curses and oaths.** Catalians swear by mirnas, the Ancients, and “the abyss”—“I swear by the abyss,” “I swear by the Ancients,” “oh, abyss”—and curse with “a thousand devils”; Red most often invokes a crab: “may a crab eat you.” These expressions echo the former faith; see [[drevnie|Ancients]].

**Names.** Names have a Spanish structure: a personal name followed by a family name, often with a particle—da Silva, da Vira, di Berro, el Niña, Ramires da Rimini, Gereira, Flarbo, Salam, Martinez, Marques, Maduro. Not everything sounds Spanish: “Deserey,” “Ir-Reya,” and “Enhal” do not resemble Spanish names; the novel does not explain this, and it is a reader's observation. The author gives “Alvara do Silva” as the correct pronunciation, while noting that he began learning Spanish recently. For more about the models, see [[prototipy|Models and Influences]].

**How it sounds.** Dangarians find Catalian “funny”: [[greta|Greta]] laughs at it in ch. 12.

### The Form of Address “Ma Donna” (ch. 28)
In ch. 28, it is explained that a lady may be addressed as “ma donna” instead of “señora” or by name and title. This is how a man several ranks below her speaks, or one who has come to ask an enormous favor.

### The First Judge's Aphorisms (ch. 22)
The floor slabs in the Hall of the Scroll bear the inscriptions “Skill is the key,” “What belongs to everyone belongs to no one,” “Honor matters more than morality,” and “One equals seven.” These are translations: the original wording is absent from the text; see [[pervyj-sudya|First Judge]].

## Dangarian
The language of [[dangar|Dangar]], spoken by both free people and slaves of the empire. In the text, it looks like German: words are taken from German and written in Russian letters, while the surrounding Russian grammar is retained. The narrator describes its sound: to Red, it is “like the cawing of a crow with a cold” (ch. 8); to Beatriz, it whistles and caws (ch. 22).

**Address and politeness.**
- **Herr, Frau**—“mister, madam”: Herr Lieutenant, Herr Staff Captain, Herr Major, Herr Baron, Frau Elsa, Frau Gieseke. The prefix **von** marks an eagle; see [[orly|Eagles and Hares]].
- **Ja, nein, bitte, danke schön, vielen Dank, guten Tag, morgen** (“tomorrow”; in ch. 12, Yakka says goodbye with this word, and Red understands that his release is “tomorrow already”).
- **“May your hand be steady.”** The eagles' greeting in place of “hello” (ch. 6, 14).

**Terms for the social order.**
- **Stein**—“stone,” a slave, the same word used for the estate: [[kamni|Stones]]. Joriksen tells the Catalian, “you are my stone” (ch. 4).
- **Zustand**—a working condition imposed on a slave; **kein Zustand**—“no conditions,” which is how the scribe announces in ch. 4 that Red has no Zustand. See [[tsushtand|Zustand]].
- **Fertig**—“ready”: a slave answers an officer with this word at the door of the residence (ch. 4). **Graben**—“to dig”; **komme**—“come, let's go” (ch. 4, 12).
- **Schönbrun**—“beautiful well,” a well containing a miracle: [[kolodets|Schönbrun]]; **dark schönbrun**—[[temnyj-shyonbrun|Dark Schönbrun]].
- **Fischmeister**—a master fisherman, a Guild member: [[fishmajster|Fischmeister]], [[gildiya-rybakov|Fishermen’s Guild]].
- **Sorge um dich**—“we care for you”: the words of the feeding ritual, spoken by free people as they place the last spoonful of porridge into a slave's mouth (ch. 8, 14). Yakka also says this to Red.
- **Gehai**—“sacred”: the name of the month when the Wunde dries up (ch. 10). The narrator explains the names **Witwen** (“Widows' District”) and **Schlissel** (“key”) in the course of the text.
- **Bestia**—“beast”: Baron Landau calls Red this after the lost debate—“not a man, but a beast” (ch. 15).
- **Scheiße, Schwerscheiße**—strong language used by Staff Captain von Zeidvist and free people (ch. 2, 4, 15).

**Names.** Names sound German: von Dort, von Lurd, von Walbrecht, von Eyring, Hofmeyer, Kurtzleben, Reiniger, Landau, Klein, Gieseke, Zeidvist. Northern names sound Scandinavian: Fritjof Joriksen and Yakka, called “Yako” in the stream (see [[yakka|Yakka]]). Slaves have no surnames, and their nicknames are given in Russian: Killnot, [[spasenie|Salvation]], [[siplyj|Hoarse]], [[boroda-lopatoj|Spadebeard]]. Milosh, although a slave, has a Slavic name ([[milosh|Milosh]]); the novel does not explain its origin.

**How Dangarians speak Catalian.** When Dangarians speak a foreign language, the author conveys this through uninflected verbs. Fischmeister Joriksen reads from a slip of paper and a dictionary: the Russian original has “Ты есть каталиетц” (“You is Catallian”) and “Я надеяться на ты” (“I to hope in you”) (ch. 4). In Yakka's speech, foreign words acquire an “-a” ending: “Рэда,” “каталиеса,” “бригадира”—“Reda,” “Cataliesa,” “foremana” (ch. 15). The reader hears the accent without needing a translation.

### Words from Later Chapters (ch. 29)
- **Täuschung**—“deception”: Baron Landau uses this word to announce that Red's well is no schönbrun, merely an ordinary pit of water (ch. 23, 27).
- **Unsicher**—“unreliable”: the term for conquered nobles whose lands are confiscated (ch. 29); see [[dangar|Dangar]].
- **“Herr Stein.”** The terrified scribe addresses a slave as “Mister Stone” (ch. 27).
- **Klein speaks Catalian.** Lieutenant [[klyajn|Klein]] taught himself Catalian from dictionaries and foreign newspapers, believing war with Catalia inevitable, and says to Red in ch. 27: “Ты не смочь таким путем” in the Russian original (“You not be able this way”).

## Ollandian
The language of [[ollandiya|Ollandia]]. It is not reproduced at all in the text: the narrator merely notes that people “speak Ollandian” and conveys the meaning of their remarks. In the spring stream, the author said Ollandian is “more or less” Polish, but that he had not thought about it; he says he chooses Slavic names: [[olkha|Olkha]], described in the novel as “Helga in your language,” and [[lents|Wladyslaw Lenz]]. The names of voivodeships and cities—Latsen, Plyutva, Mershants, Ginarva, Bargot—likewise sound neither German nor Spanish; the novel gives no explanations for them.
- In ch. 6, Dietrich does not know Ollandian and cannot speak to the girl at the neighboring table.
- The Ollandian girl initially pretends not to understand Dangarian and mangles her words: the Russian original reads “Кто вий сказать? Я не понимайт” (“Who you say? I no understand”). She then speaks it tolerably well: Olkha studied for five years at Saint Adelia's boarding school in Hofstadt (ch. 17).
- Prince Lenz speaks Dangarian “with the slightest accent” (ch. 14).

### The Magnus's Test (ch. 29)
Magnus [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]] says something in Ollandian to test Dietrich, who replies that he does not know the language (ch. 29).

## Muari and Solantan
The languages of the [[muaris|Muari Emirates]] and the island of [[solanta|Solanta]]. There are no samples of speech at the beginning of the novel: all we know is that [[filippe|Filippe di Berro's]] servant, a Muari in a white tailcoat, speaks “with an accent” (ch. 3). [[red|Red]] knows both languages: in ch. 4, hoping there has been a translation error in his agreement with the fischmeister, he asks him again in Muari and Solantan, but Joriksen knows neither.

### Fatima's Name (ch. 22)
Beatriz finds the name of the half-Solantan [[fatima|Fatima Ir-Reya]] strange and detects no Solantan features in it: “It does not sound like Solantan either” (ch. 22). The name remains unexplained, while the Muari sorcerer Faid-Musa bears an Arabic-sounding name—a reader's observation.

## Merganan (ch. 32)
The language of [[mergana|Mergana]]. According to Beatriz, it resembles Dangarian (ch. 26) and uses the same word, “schönbrun,” for a Well. Beatriz knows Merganan (ch. 24, 26). The philosopher [[liess-van-khalm|Liess van Halm]] is Merganan by birth, but in ch. 26 his scribbles do not resemble Merganan either. According to a theory put forward by one of the characters in ch. 32, van Halm spoke Dangarian.

## A Foreign Accent in ch. 2 (ch. 33)
In ch. 2, Dietrich hears a foreign accent in a stone soldier's speech: the man mixes up words and explains that he never fires. By the end of the novel, the soldier's identity becomes clear; see [[red|Red]].
