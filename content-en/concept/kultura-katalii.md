This article is about how Catalians live, rather than how their state is organized: the system of esferas and the High Table is described in [[kataliya|Catalia]]. In the preface and opening chapters, the narrator presents the country through the eyes of the writer [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]], bringing the spoken word, evening entertainment, art, and money to the fore. In interviews, the author called Catalia a “triumph of materialism” and a society of individualists, “people of the word”; examples from the novel put both descriptions to the test.

## Life by esfera
For a Catalian, profession is the chief marker of belonging. An [[esfera|esfera]] determines a person’s city district, daily schedule—for people of Art, eight in the morning is still “the middle of the night”—bracelet, and circle of acquaintances. In ch. 7, the narrator observes that the Three Bulls, Craft, Agriculture, and Service, feed, clothe, and clean up after the country, while in conversation over a mug of beer, workers call the other eight esferas “parasites.” This is visible in the restaurant too: as an unfamiliar gentleman reminds the protagonist, the establishment near the Grand Opera is “for the higher ranks of Art and Law,” yet Alvaro has brought his obrero friends there (ch. 1).

According to the author, the Catalian dream resembles the American one: to rise through hard work from apprentice to maestro and don. This is reflected in the rule “Skill is the key”: a title is not inherited, but must be earned and confirmed in a disputation. The exception is factory ownership in the esfera of Production: a factory passes by inheritance, and complaints about this never cease in the [[tri-byka|Three Bulls]] tavern (ch. 5, 13). For bracelets as signs of rank, see [[braslet|Bracelets]].

## Words instead of weapons
Catalians call themselves people of the word. A conflict is settled in a verbal duel in the [[palata-slova|Chamber of the Word]], before an arbiter and at least five jurors ([[duelnyj-spor|Dueling Debate]]). Jurors serve without pay and come for the spectacle, so the Chamber is jokingly called a “free theater” (ch. 1). A debate is structured as a performance: in ch. 1, Alvaro enters one without preparing any “ingenious arguments,” as he admits himself.

Catalia treats speech tolerantly (ch. 13): the detective [[diego-ingres|Diego Ingres]] explains to Alvaro that freedom of speech is the most important human right in Catalia, so even a verbal threat is not punishable, and any voluntary transaction is legal. The person threatened has the right to respond personally, including by issuing a challenge to debate or duel; the law intervenes only when measurable harm has been caused.

## The Scroll and fame
Every Catalian hopes for an entry in the [[svitok|Great Scroll]]. For a writer, this is the highest fame: Alvaro dreams of inscribing his name on the Scroll as his grandfather [[rikardo-flores|Ricardo Flores]], author of “The Snake in the Reeds,” once did. Victory in debate turns an opinion into truth. If jurors declare a novel a masterpiece, the author’s heirs gain the right to demand reprints (ch. 22). Literary fame in Catalia thus looks like a legal category: jurors, as well as critics, determine a book’s worth. Newspapers such as “[[vestnik|The Herald]]” serve as a public arena.

## Art
- **Opera.** The [[grand-opera|Grand Opera]] is the center of evening life: before the premiere of “Señorita Violet,” a queue forms by its steps, and during the interval, audiences debate the ending in the refreshment rooms. The opera’s hero, the hidalgo Don Laurent, seduces increasingly highborn ladies; traditionally, the heroine stabs herself with a knife out of anguish. As a performer, Don [[filippe|Filippe]] secured a different ending. Most of the audience also liked its moral: dying for love is the last thing one should do (ch. 3).
- **Gallery.** The [[galereya-eliste|Eliste Gallery]] holds a collection spanning three centuries, and statues of mirnas stand on its roof. Laertans have tired of the permanent collection, and the gallery has acquired a reputation as a “museum for students and foreigners,” so the trustees have devoted an entire floor to scandalous temporary exhibitions (ch. 16).
- **Academy.** The [[akademiya-iskusstv|Academy of Arts]] teaches the craft, and its students wear identical uniforms (ch. 3). A writer and lecturer such as Alvaro gives lectures about plot.
- **Books and cafés.** [[khose-flarbo|Jose Flarbo’s]] shop and [[malena|Malena’s]] café are places where novels are discussed; in the restaurant, Alvaro dines to Selezian romances that are at once passionate and melancholy (ch. 1).

## Mirnas, oaths, and faith
Catalians invoke the [[mirny|mirnas]] and the [[drevnie|Ancients]] in oaths and curses: “I swear by the abyss,” “the mirnas are my witnesses,” “glory to the mirnas.” Mirnas are patrons of occupations; their figurines move around the clock on the Academy tower, and alguacils wear [[iolanta|Iolanta’s]] white feather in their hats. The author compared the Catalians’ attitude to gods with an attitude to muses: they have no icons or prayers; only words and images remain.

## Rituals
- **Funerals.** A dry [[sinkho|sinkho]] tree is placed on the porch of the deceased’s house, and anyone may tie a ribbon to it: a pale or blue one for fond memories, red or black otherwise. The expression “to go into the blue beyond” means to die (ch. 5). The author stressed that this tradition contains no mockery.
- **Marriage and divorce.** In an interview, the author explained that Catalians try to find something good in every event and rejoice at weddings, divorces, and even funerals. Divorce requires a “right of divorce,” which is used up.

### Divorce is a celebration (ch. 11)
In ch. 11, Alvaro and Fernan pounce on [[ugo-martines|Ugo Martinez]], a man they mistake for someone tailing them. He explains that he has just divorced his wife: his “right of divorce” has been spent, but it was worth it. The woman takes off her hat, and grains of rice fall from her hair; she and the children sing: “Happiness and freedom!” At first the protagonists do not understand whom they should congratulate; the rice, as at a wedding, shows that this is a genuine celebration.

## Food and wine
- Among the country’s exports, the narrator names “tart wine and snow-white rice” (ch. 7). Rice is a staple of everyday cooking: the Three Bulls tavern serves fried rice with beef (ch. 5). **Paella** is eaten both in restaurants (ch. 1) and at home: in ch. 20, Lucille brings it to the sick Alvaro.
- In an expensive restaurant, Alvaro orders a bottle of red **Alan wine**, “one of the strongest on the menu”; the Gereiras traditionally drink their first toast to love (ch. 1).
- **Cofenado** is a dark drink in a small bottle, which Lucille uses to reconcile the friends (ch. 9). **Bonk** is a strong drink: Red says that it was used to suppress his cough, and Dr. Juanes was given a ten-year-old Almagarian bonk (ch. 4, 5). Tea with biscuits and coffee on the balcony form part of Alvaro’s domestic life (ch. 1, 13).
- The restaurants around the Grand Opera compete on price (ch. 1).

## Clothing and signs
Catalians wear bracelets that immediately reveal their esfera and rank; these can be hidden beneath a sleeve (ch. 16). Academy students wear uniforms. Among Catalian goods, the narrator names “leather boots and colorful linen dresses” (ch. 7). Guards of the esfera of [[teni|Shadow]] are distinguished by a red scarf tied around their hair “in pirate fashion” and a thick beard without a mustache, while alguacils wear a white feather in their hats (ch. 13).

## Foreigners and slavery (ch. 32)
Foreigners are common in Laerta, but they are the first to fall under suspicion: if a “white corpse” is found on white land, the first person sought is a foreigner, because a Catalian would not kill on such ground ([[belaya-zemlya|White, Grey, and Black Land]]); the quartermaster [[zolotoj-dukat|Golden Ducat]] explains that not one of the forty foreigners in el Niña’s crew has ever spilled blood “beyond a white pillar” (ch. 18). There is no slavery in Catalia: in ch. 32, the protagonists are told that the slave trade was “banned long ago” in both Catalia and Muaris. The occasion is an old story about a dice game in which a losing Dangarian wagered an enslaved woman: the girl was returned to the sultan’s harem, and the winner was arrested. For a Catalian who becomes one of the [[kamni|stones]], the loss of “ordinary human respect,” regarded in Catalia as an inalienable right, is especially keen: [[red|Red]] experiences this in ch. 23. Dangarians, for their part, call Catalians “windbags.”

## Piracy as a trade (ch. 7–9)
One of the esferas, [[teni|Shadow]], includes thieves, swindlers, assassins, prostitutes, and pirates. In ch. 7, the narrator lists specializations: buccaneers, privateers, state-commissioned raiders, and corsairs. Pirates plunder foreign ships and ports and, as Count [[lurd|Lurd]] observes, leave ships traveling to and from Catalia alone (ch. 17). Carlos el Niña started on a corsair schooner, then found a more profitable line of work: smuggling. Catalia has neither customs nor duties, and trade is considered beneficial, so smugglers bring goods from countries that impose duties on them. In the “black district,” the [[staryj-port|Old Port]], anyone with a pirate’s or assassin’s bracelet need not fear punishment for practicing their trade. Catalia recognizes piracy as a trade rather than a crime: it has its own bracelet.

## The concept
In interviews, the author stressed that Catalia is not a copy of Spain: its “style, naming system, and clothing” are Spanish, but its structure is its own. He described rituals such as a joyful divorce as a philosophy of “finding something positive in every event” and did not intend to ridicule it. See also [[slovo-avtora|The Author’s Words]].
