The Great Scroll is the central repository of records in [[kataliya|Catalia]]. Every Catalian hopes to leave a mark upon it: the writer [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]] dreams of inscribing his name on the Scroll as his grandfather [[rikardo-flores|Ricardo Flores]], author of “The Snake in the Reeds,” once did. The names of the heroes of Solanta are entered on the Scroll as the names of heroes (ch. 3).

## What is entered on the Scroll (ch. 22)
The Scroll records not the population but Truth: whatever has prevailed in a debate before jurors ([[duelnyj-spor]]). If a publisher and a critic debate whether a novel is a masterpiece, and the jurors rule that it is, an entry appears on the Scroll and acquires the force of law: the author’s heirs gain the right to demand reprints, and teachers may assign it to students. Personal truths are entered in the same way: the award of bracelets and titles, the defense of examination work, marriages and divorces, adoption, crimes, honors and their forfeiture, the question of who should pay to bury an unloved relative, and much more (ch. 22).

An entry can be challenged only through a new debate: whoever defeats all the previous arguments changes Truth. If no claim is brought, the entry remains. That is why the Scroll is called the foundation of Catalian faith in the power of the word.

## How the Scroll works (ch. 22)
The Scroll’s building is both temple and court, in a valley between three cliffs in northern [[laerta|Laerta]]. A statue of the [[pervyj-sudya|First Judge]] stands before the entrance; his principles are engraved on the marble floor slabs: “Skill is the key,” “What belongs to everyone belongs to no one,” “Honor matters more than morality,” “One equals seven.” The Scroll physically exists as a scroll: it consists of rolls of paper a thousand yards long, with a few inches allocated to each entry. When a roll is full, it is taken to an underground repository and joined to a new one with a certified strip. This rules out forgery: forging an entry would require cutting through the middle of a roll, which would be visible. Steam engines stand in the hall, and secretaries provide reference information.

A visitor receives a card with a sequence of signs—a star, a small square, a figure eight, a slash—and goes to a reading room: a cell with a desk and a brass machine with a viewing window, two levers marked “forward” and “back,” and buttons bearing symbols. Entering the key makes the machine display the records.

## The Scroll in the investigation (ch. 13, 22)
Guards and [[algvasily|alguacils]] work with the “letter of the Scroll”: guards intervene when a crime is obvious, while alguacils establish how far the Scroll has been violated (ch. 13). In ch. 22, Alvaro and [[beatris|Beatriz]] go to the Hall of the Scroll to learn about [[red|Rodrigo da Vira]] and [[fatima|Fatima]]. The records tell Alvaro that da Vira was a hot-tempered brawler who often went on trial, but won a debate for the title of master navigator. Three months after the Solantan expedition, el Niña and Fatima won the case in which da Vira was posthumously awarded the Order of Courage; later, [[karmensita-estevos|Carmensita Estevos]] unsuccessfully sought recognition as his widow and recognition of her son as his child. Carmensita was refused for lack of evidence, and disappeared.

## What can be recorded (ch. 20)
Fernan explains to Alvaro that, according to entries on the Scroll, any voluntary transaction is legal in Catalia. Rumors, seawater, and a body part—an actress pledged her right leg—have all been used as collateral. Factory owners and bankers may therefore agree to anything, provided the other party consents.

## The concept and interviews
The author names the First Judge as the creator of the Great Scroll and founder of the system of dueling debates; he promises to explain more about them later (interview iv1).
