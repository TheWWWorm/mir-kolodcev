The Sower is Dangar’s one god. Dangarians call him the All-Good and depict him in icons and frescoes as a barefoot man in peasant clothes, striding across a field and scattering grain. Golden ears of wheat rise behind him, eagles soar above him, fluffy hares cluster around his feet, and stones lie in rows along the sides of the field. They all open their mouths as though asking for food. This image summarizes the entire Dangarian philosophy of society: the three estates—[[orly|eagles]], hares, and [[kamni|stones]]—descend from the “good souls” who answered the Sower’s call.

## The parable of the field
Dietrich retells the parable in ch. 6. To create humanity, the Sower chose an untamed field. He began to plow, but the ground was stony, and he called: “Help me, good souls!” The stones came to life and moved aside. He began to weed, but the weeds grew thickly, so he called again: hares came running and dug them up with their little paws. He began to sow, but small rodents ate the grain; eagles flew down and exterminated the rodents. Thus the estates arose from those who had answered. But the wheat grew poorly because there was not enough water. Then the Sower dug a well in which faith in a miracle splashed, scooped up that faith and watered the field, and the wheat came into ear. From this, Dangarians conclude that even the finest words bear no fruit without faith in a miracle, and that this faith lives in Beautiful Wells ([[kolodets|schönbruns]]). The grains in the parable are holy words.

## Doctrine and rituals (ch. 2–33)
- **Love for the stones.** According to scripture, of all living souls the stones are most cherished by the Sower’s love: he has freed them from the burden of responsibility for their actions, the agony of choice, and the uncertainty of finding their path and place in life. Neither hares nor eagles enjoy such happiness (ch. 2).
- **Care.** The Sower’s will is that people should care for one another. After a church service, free people spoon-feed porridge to the slaves, saying “Sorge um dich”—“I care for you” (ch. 19); at a noble banquet, eagles stand behind their stone servants and put the last spoonful into their mouths, saying “We care for you” (ch. 14). Dietrich explains that in the “Silver Kingdom,” everyone fed one another, and the custom remained with the stones as their victory, won by arms in the Wars of the Three Kingdoms.
- **Water.** The saying “Water is God’s little sister” connects water with the Sower (ch. 2, 14).
- **Churches.** Wooden churches with white-stone façades and a silver bird above the entrance stand at every station (ch. 4, 8). The priest wears a broad gold bracer, while his assistants wear grey cassocks (ch. 8).
- **Executions.** Slaves are executed in church with a bullet in the back of the head on the altar stone; the stone receives the blood, while the soul is believed to fly to the Sower (ch. 33).

## The Sower and the wells (ch. 15, 19, 23)
Joriksen is convinced that the Sower created the wells and therefore hopes that the Catalian, selected in accordance with Red’s essence, will repeat the miracle. Red knows a different story: according to Catalian traditions, the Ancients created the Wells. From the Catalian’s point of view, the Sower is a sorcerer, not a god, which means that a human also has a chance.

In the church fresco at Dunkwald, the Sower digs a well himself with a shovel; a pickaxe lies nearby, with hares, birds, and stones all around. Red notices that the Sower is not singing—his mouth is closed, and the hares’ ears are lowered—and doubts whether songs are necessary. His friend [[greta|Greta]] is convinced that the Sower himself chose Red, and explains this through a drawing (ch. 23). A temple slave whom Red addresses through gestures corrects the scoffer: it was the Sower who dug, and Red was only his instrument. Red grows angry and leaves, later realizing that he had been frightened.

## The Sower’s brand (ch. 33)
Church slaves are branded with the Sower’s name. The brand heals letter by letter: the first letters disappear, then the middle ones fade, and when the brand is entirely gone, the slave may become a priest. If the slave despairs, the brand stops healing. As ch. 25 indicates, Dangar’s priesthood is recruited chiefly from former stones.

## Power and faith (ch. 17–29)
In ch. 17, Count Ludwig von Lurd names three centers of influence: the emperor, the patriarch, and the magnus of the Fishermen’s Guild. Through the church and the Sower, the patriarch influences the stones’ minds no less than the kaiser. According to the magnus, the kaiser dines with the patriarch on Tuesdays and Saturdays (ch. 29).

## The concept and interviews
According to the author, Dangar is a monotheistic country, with churches everywhere, even in the wilderness, and religion together with wells forms the foundation of Dangarian philosophy. Catalia, by contrast, has never had monotheism, and its gods are regarded “as we regard muses.” The custom by which masters feed the stones by hand is, the author says, a privilege the stones “won” themselves; he promised to tell its history in later books, along with the origins of slavery.
