In “Traces on the Water,” numbers always play a role in the story. Catalians reckon in escudos and Dangarians in marks, and a sum of money often sets events in motion. The best-known example is the seven thousand escudos that [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]] loses in a debate.

## Units and means of payment
- **The escudo** is Catalia’s principal currency. Large stakes are specified as “in gold”—“a thousand escudos in gold”—while banknotes circulate in everyday life.
- **Pesetas and pesos.** Small change: in ch. 3, the narrator speaks of “the last few pesetas wrapped in a rag,” while Alvaro and [[fernan-gerejra|Fernan]] complain that they do not have “a peso” in their pockets. The text does not explain the relationship between the peseta, peso, and escudo.
- **Promissory notes and IOUs.** A debtor has the right to issue a promissory note, but it is not accepted everywhere: innkeepers are happy to put a debt on a tab, but the postal service does not accept IOUs (ch. 13). The bank maintains personal accounts.
- **Collateral in the Scroll.** According to the records of the [[svitok|Great Scroll]], any voluntary transaction is legal: rumors, seawater, and even a body part have been used as collateral (ch. 20).
- **The mark** is the principal currency of [[dangar|Dangar]]. There are ten-mark banknotes and “full-weight silver marks” (ch. 10, 14, 21).

## The stake in the dueling debate (ch. 1–3)
When [[vinsente-ramires|Vincente Ramires]] insults Alvaro’s friends, Alvaro proposes a stake of a thousand escudos in gold. Ramires refuses: he wants a stake that will make his opponent feel the defeat, so he wagers a month’s income. The arbiter rules that Alvaro’s monthly income is seven thousand escudos, because that is the amount he received that month for the publication of his book; he spent fifteen months writing the novel, but the fee counts as income on the day it is paid. He is given three days to pay ([[duelnyj-spor-ramiresa|more details]]).

At the bank, Alvaro discovers that he has only four thousand six hundred escudos in his account. In a single month, he has spent a third of his fee. He covers the shortfall of more than two thousand with a promissory note due within no more than three months (ch. 3). He does not expect his next fee for at least a year.

That same evening, [[filippe|Filippe di Berro]] offers him a commission: a book about the heroes of Solanta. He promises to cover the debt and pay another three thousand escudos for the book’s outline, then another three thousand for the finished text (ch. 3). When Alvaro sends a letter declining the commission, a furious Fernan shakes him: “Seven thousand down the cesspit—the second lot this month.” This is how his friend values the lost commission (ch. 13): the terms in ch. 3 were stated differently—the debt plus two payments of three thousand—and only Fernan gives the figure of seven thousand. He himself, he says, lives on a hundred escudos a month.

## Earnings and prices in Laerta (ch. 13–32)
- **Payments for various services.** The prize for deciphering Cantuseño’s paintings is a thousand escudos (ch. 16). “The Philosophy of Horror” costs two escudos in [[khose-flarbo|Flarbo’s]] shop (ch. 16). Losing a hundred escudos at cards is a minor annoyance by Carlos el Niña’s standards (ch. 18).
- **A thousand escudos.** Alvaro talks [[dezerej|Deserey]] into giving him a thousand escudos “for business.” To him, it is a large sum (ch. 30).
- **Guides’ fees and the cost of visits.** At the [[klinika-miloserdiya|Mercy Clinic]], a visit to [[liess-van-khalm|van Halm]] costs twenty escudos; [[beatris|Beatriz]] pays (ch. 26). Watching pyromaniacs set fire to everything in sight costs five escudos, and hearing the philosopher’s story costs two. In Tres Molinos, [[khavyer-salam|Javier Salam]] charges twenty escudos for a tour of van Halm’s house and another five for the truth about the philosopher’s servants. He values information about suspicious visitors at five escudos for the couple in love and ten for the “villains” (ch. 32).

## The price of a person (ch. 4)
Red reasons that he was bought at auction “for a considerable sum” in another country: the slave trader inflated the price by giving him bonk before the auction to suppress his cough, and the journey by ship and railway carriage added to the cost. The novel gives no specific amount.

## Dangar’s marks (ch. 10–21)
The budget of [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort’s]] family is shown in detail in ch. 10.
- The family’s savings amount to 293 marks. A month’s living costs 60–70 marks, depending on the price of water and firewood. Mari complains that milk, bread, and candles have cost half a mark.
- A ticket for [[rybalka-valbrekht|fishing at Baroness Walbrecht’s]] costs two hundred marks. Dietrich buys one from Frau Gieseke for twenty ten-mark banknotes (ch. 10, 14). The fishing guests insist that the baroness has charged too little and that “love is worth a thousand.” Joriksen, meanwhile, says that one client promised him a million marks “after the catch” (ch. 14).
- The rate for the services of Master [[joriksen|Joriksen]] is given in ch. 21: five hundred silver marks for a day of getting to know a schönbrun and another five hundred for a day of fishing—ten thousand a month excluding days off. The contract for ten thousand “full-weight marks” states that Dietrich will be released from payment if he allows Joriksen access to his schönbrun. He has no way to pay the sum: he has only one mark left in his pocket.
- Dietrich’s salary in Joriksen’s service is three hundred marks a month, and he receives his first payment immediately. He sends a hundred and fifty to his wife in an envelope.
- Count Wahrheit holds the bank for a thousand marks. Plans to build a railway in Ollandia are discussed in marks per verst of track (ch. 14).

## State expenditures (ch. 28)
General Deserey estimates that mobilizing another two hundred thousand soldiers would cost three hundred million escudos for the first month and a hundred million for each subsequent month. This is the “extra” that will have to be “squeezed out of all the esferas,” as Alvaro’s companion puts it. For comparison, in ch. 9 Deserey gives the strength of the Catalian army as two hundred and seventy thousand men.
