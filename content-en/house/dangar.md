Dangar is the empire where [[ditrikh|Dietrich von Dort]] was born and where the Catalian [[red|Red]] finds himself in the novel. Life here is entirely subject to rank and faith: noble eagles serve the Kaiser out of honor, free hares administer and trade, enslaved [[kamni|stones]] perform almost all the heavy labor, and above everyone stands the [[seyatel|Sower]], in whose name the church cares even for those it brands. The empire is constantly waging war and expanding; at the start of the novel, it is conquering Ollandia. The author describes Dangar, like Catalia, as the embodiment of an idea of the state and sees no need to “root for” a country: his sympathies lie with people.

## Land and cities
The empire occupies the west of the known world: great forests, rivers, slow railways, and scattered cities. As chapter 4 describes it, a traveler arriving at a port on the Kobold Sea is met by coastal forts and strict inspections, then spends weeks on a westbound train. Stations become fewer, every little town has a church, villages are impoverished and silent, and trains stop running in winter because the water in their tanks freezes. The terminus is a small town with a white church; beyond it, there are no tracks. On the author’s map, Dangar is the largest region on the left, with mountains to the north and northwest.

The capital is [[khofshtadt|Hofstadt]], on the River Wunde, with about two million inhabitants. Its heart is the Schlissel district, with ten ministries, the general staff headquarters, the patriarchal cathedral, the treasury, the Life Guards’ barracks, and a star-shaped palace with ten towers. West of the capital, the author’s map marks [[ejring|Eyring]] (“the ancestral seat of those very von Eyrings”); [[chinkhar|Chinkhar]] is labeled in the northwest. Beyond the western edge of the map, Red serves his sentence in [[dunkvald|Dunkwald]].

## The estates
Society rests on three estates:
- **Eagles** are nobles with the prefix “von.” They serve out of honor: resignation would mean disgrace. An eagle without land or stones can only become an officer, and Dietrich admits that during the siege of Plyutva he prayed for an officer’s commission. Nobility of birth is measured in generations: some of the Kaiser’s Claws still had “hares” for grandfathers. See [[orly|Eagles and Hares]].
- **Hares** are free commoners and the middle class: merchants, managers, clerks, and literate “free people.” Employment in government offices requires a noble’s recommendation.
- **Stones** are slaves. They wear a small stone on a chain as a sign that the Sower loves them, and a brand on their bodies; without work, they receive no food. The words “THOU SHALT NOT KILL” branded into the body make a slave incapable of killing.

## Power (ch. 6, 17)
In chapter 17, Count [[lurd|Ludwig von Lurd]] explains that Dangar has three centers of influence: the Kaiser and the army; the patriarch, who governs the clergy and influences the stones through the church; and the magnus of the fischmeisters’ guild, which sells miracles to the new nobility. The magnus is “the third man in the empire.” In an interview, the author confirmed that the Kaiser, patriarch, and chief magnus interact, and that their relationships will be an important subject of the next book; court affairs remain outside Traces on the Water.

The Kaiser controls a special service, the [[kogti-kajzera|Kaiser’s Claws]]: counterintelligence and a military department that certifies officers’ promotions. The Claws receive orders directly and wear a silver falcon patch; their highest distinction is a red phoenix patch. The foreign ministry is effectively run by its deputy minister, Count von Lurd.

## Details of the social order (ch. 21–33)
Slaveholders pay the army a head tax of one stone in a hundred each year (ch. 33). A debtor without noble status can pledge a loved one as a stone (ch. 21). The clergy is recruited from stones (ch. 25): a slave bearing a brand of the Sower’s name can become a priest if he does not despair; the brand heals one letter at a time (ch. 33). Executions take place in church, with a shot to the back of the head on the altar stone, which receives the blood.

## Religion
Dangarians are deeply religious, and, as the author remarked, Dangarian philosophy contains “more profound thoughts.” Their single god is the [[seyatel|Sower]]. Churches are everywhere, a silver bird spreading its wings over each entrance; inside are icons of suffering and sacrifice. According to the parable (ch. 6), the Sower created the estates from “good souls”: the stones moved aside, the hares dug up the weeds, and the eagles exterminated the rodents, while the seeds were holy words. The well filled with faith in miracles is the source of water, which is why Beautiful Wells are sacred. Scripture says that stones are the souls most cherished by the Sower’s love: they are relieved of responsibility, choice, and doubt.

Feeding is a special ritual. Outside the church, free people feed slaves porridge, saying “Sorge um dich” (“we care for you”), and place the final spoonful in their mouths. Stones won this privilege by force of arms in the Wars of the Three Kingdoms (ch. 14), and it is now considered a virtue. The sacred River Wunde dries up for a month known as Gehai.

## Wells and the Fishermen’s Guild (ch. 4–27)
The empire’s religious and practical life is inseparable from wells. In Dangarian, a miraculous well is called a [[kolodets|schönbrun]] (“beautiful well”), while masters who know how to “fish” in it are [[fishmajster|fischmeisters]]. They belong to a guild ([[gildiya-rybakov|Fishermen’s Guild]]) headed by the magnus; its members wear azure cloaks fastened with a golden catfish-shaped brooch. The guild maintains an atlas of all known wells and seeks a way to create a schönbrun: to this end, enslaved stones are forced to dig wells under special conditions ([[tsushtand|Zustand]]), individually tailored to each person’s nature. The principal testing ground is the settlement of [[dunkvald|Dunkwald]], founded half a century earlier. No new schönbrun has been discovered in roughly fifty years. The society fishing event at Baroness [[elza|Elsa von Walbrecht’s]] is an expensive amusement for nobles.

## The army (ch. 2, 6, 9, 31)
The army is divided along the same lines as society: officers are eagles and ordinary soldiers are stones. Stones obey orders without thought and are steadfast but unwieldy, ill-suited to mountain warfare and independent operations; the cavalry consists of eagles. Weapons include smoothbore fusils without front sights, six-pounder cannon, mortars with a range of about one and a half versts, and heavy siege mortars. Troops are supplied by rail, and building tracks instead of fortresses is a sign of offensive warfare. Ranks include staff captain, lieutenant, noncommissioned officer, feldwebel, colonel, general of infantry, and field marshal. The customary greeting is “May your hand be steady” (“A steady hand to you”). “Special” slaves also play a military role: those branded across the chest with “THOU SHALT NOT KILL” using a red-hot chain are incapable of killing.

## The war with Ollandia (ch. 2–6, 28, 31)
Dangar conquers [[ollandiya|Ollandia]] along the railway: [[plyutva|Plyutva]] has fallen, with ten thousand Ollandians branded as stones, as has [[latsen|Latsen]]. By chapter 31, the armies of Generals [[kurtsleben|Kurtzleben]], [[brojenshtoff|Brojenstoff]], and [[khofmajer|Hofmeyer]] are positioned in the country’s center, in the south by the River [[khursla|Khursla]], and outside [[mershants|Mershants]] Fortress near the Catalian border. River supplies to the southern army failed because [[mergana|Mergana]] would not allow the steamships through; the sole Plyutva–Mershants railway branch cannot meet demand. Field Marshal [[rajniger|Reiniger]] reallocates forces, while Major [[klyajn|Klein]] of the Kaiser’s Claws searches for a “schönbrun of victory” for Hofmeyer. See [[olandskaya-vojna|The Ollandian War]] and [[tri-zalpa|The Battle at the Well on the Hill]].

The empire’s policy toward conquered peoples is called “unsicher”: the conquered country’s nobility is divided into the reliable, who retain their lands and their people’s freedom, and the unreliable, who lose their lands and have between twenty and seventy percent of their people branded as slaves (ch. 29). The author described Ollandia’s assimilation in these terms: some peasants are bound into servitude and made stones, the middle class becomes hares, and the nobles become eagles. The Kaiser’s governor in Ollandia is central to unification; chapter 25 says the role requires an Ollandian noble related by marriage to the clergy or the military aristocracy.

## Economy and technology
The empire is poor and conservative. Its locomotives are outdated, slaves pull carts, people fight over tips at railway stations, and luxurious carriages in Hofstadt are “human-powered.” Few horses are available. Yet the empire has cannon, steamships on the Wunde, and factory owners interested in the guild’s miracles. A curious feature of the capital is its “airplanes”: enormous three-masted airships belonging to the patriarch and the Life Guards, fitted with gunports, bells, and religious banners (ch. 10). The currency is marks and pfennigs; ten-mark banknotes are blue. Fishing with a master costs five hundred marks a day, and a ticket to the baroness’s fishing event costs two hundred. The newspaper Glory, later Glory of the Empire, reports victories and disasters.

## History (ch. 10, 14, 27)
The novel mentions only a few historical milestones. Seventy years earlier, Empress Augusta waged the Chinkhar Wars until she made peace to avoid creating more widows; the conquest of [[chinkhar|Chinkhar]] was completed thirty years later under her grandson. Stones gained the right to be fed during the Wars of the Three Kingdoms. Dunkwald was founded half a century earlier as the guild’s laboratory. One military school is called the Kaiser Ferdinand II Artillery Academy. The author said that the history of Dangar’s and Catalia’s formation, including the reasons for slavery and the stones’ civil war, would be left for later books.

## Language
Dangarian in the novel is modeled on German: “komme,” “morgen,” “praiza,” “genug,” “sorge um dich,” “Zustand,” “unsicher,” “fertig,” and “schönbrun.” Fusilier infantry, ranks and uniforms, the Kaiser, and “von” all point to a German-Prussian model. The author said that he studied German at university and that readers sometimes find errors in Dangarian words.

## Conception and interviews
The author stressed that Dangar is “not Prussia”: the language and style—ranks, clothing, and the Kaiser—are German, but its essence differs. He described the country as a tightly interconnected system. According to him, the higher estates care for the stones, while the stones’ hands produce almost everything of value: “free the stones, and everything falls apart.” He described the setting’s period as a notional “X century,” where steam engines, cannon, and slavery coexist. He is fond of Master Yurikson, the count, and his wife Katrina, and less fond of Dietrich. He compared Red’s situation in Dangar to a concentration camp.

## By the end of the novel (ch. 29–33)
By the end of the book, it emerges that Dunkwald was destroyed several months earlier: all four hundred inhabitants, including three platoons, were found dead (ch. 29); the magnus [[amir-sedoj|Amir the Grey]] demands an explanation from Joriksen. Hofmeyer’s army outside [[mershants|Mershants]] stands at the Catalian border, while Dietrich, having escaped arrest, finds himself engaged to the Ollandian princess [[olkha|Olkha Lenz]], despite already being married to [[mari|Mari]]. Army tax collectors take Red away on the recommendation of the Kaiser’s Claws (ch. 33).
