The Reformists are not a single party with a charter, but a broad movement dissatisfied with the system by which the [[vysokij-stol|High Table]] governs Catalia. It includes unemployed [[obrero|obreros]], workers from Brega and Laerta, students, small artisans, and several engineers. The movement centers on the engineer Mor, and its headquarters is the Three Bulls tavern where three districts meet, a place where obreros feel at home. To the novel’s protagonist, the Reformists are initially merely “Fernan’s promises,” then an irritating obstacle, and finally allies of necessity.

## Ideas (ch. 1–5)
In the first chapter, Fernan Gereira explains his grievances to Alvaro. Since the time of the [[pervyj-sudya|First Judge]], every [[esfera|esfera]] has had three [[alleny|allens]] at the High Table, regardless of its size: the Art esfera numbers about one hundred thousand, while the peasant esfera has at least six million, so a peasant’s vote carries tens of times less weight. Fernan and Lucille want representation to be proportional to an estate’s population. Alvaro disagrees: he considers Catalia wisely organized and believes that skill is what raises a person’s standing. The exception, as he later admits himself (ch. 13), is the Production esfera, where a don owns a factory and factories are inherited.

In chapter five, Fernan calls the rich “parasites” and “bloodsuckers” and offers to work as Alvaro’s secretary, gathering information before they “squeeze out” five or six esferas at the High Table. Alvaro agrees on one condition: no reform speeches during their meetings, and no using whatever dirt they discover for politics.

The movement’s secret side is a society with aliases and colored insignia. In chapter three, an Academy of Arts student calling himself Wasp thanks Alvaro “on behalf of all of us” for defending equality in the Chamber of the Word. He has yellow hair, a yellow ribbon in his buttonhole, and a little black tie: the “yellow ribbons” are the Reformists’ distinguishing mark.

## The Chamber debate and the railway barricade (ch. 7–11)
The first confrontation described in the novel is the obreros’ conflict with factory owners over steam-powered machinery. The owners install machines and dismiss workers; the obreros, backed by bankers from the Finance esfera, challenge the dons to a debate in the [[palata-slova|Chamber of the Word]]. The factory owners do not attend in person but send legal specialists from the Law esfera, led by [[fatima|Fatima]], and the obreros lose. They then march to Don Balboa’s arms factory, where they are beaten by guards and thugs from the Shadow esfera, while the newspapers call them “madmen with torches.” How the protesters stop a train carrying steam-powered machines from Brega, and how the fighting by the tracks ends, is described in the article on [[stachka-v-laerte|unrest in Laerta]].

## Mor and the Reformists’ “plan” (ch. 13–20)
By chapter thirteen, the Reformists are ready to ask the writer for help. Wasp delivers an invitation to come to the Three Bulls that evening: “Mor hopes you’ll help.” At the same time, Don Maduro has his guard Salvador interrogate Alvaro: what do the Reformists want? Knowing nothing of the movement’s aims, Alvaro improvises: he demands that only one of the Craft esfera’s three allens be a factory owner, with the other two drawn from the obreros and masters, and that Maduro surrender his seat to the “leader of the yellow ribbons.” Unexpectedly, both the Reformists and Maduro take this as a serious signal.

In chapter twenty, Mor personally explains her history and aims. She is a woman of about fifty, Esperanza’s chief engineer, owner of two percent of the factory, and inventor of the pressure condenser. Ten years earlier, she organized dismissed workers in Brega. They lost their debate with the factory owners—“whoever owns the machines makes the rules”—then mortgaged their homes and built their own factory for two thousand workers. After three years it began operating; after four it turned a profit. When the crisis reached Laerta, local obreros asked for help, but the factory owners hired legal specialists, defeated the bankers in the Chamber of the Word, and the banks could no longer offer workers preferential loans. Mor wants a new debate, but victory requires removing the Law esfera from the contest: persuading Fatima to forbid her subordinates from representing the factory owners. This is why Mor seeks Alvaro out: he is thought capable of approaching the prim-allen.

At first, Mor frightens her listener with a “plan”: close ten factories, seize the Production esfera, ban steam engines, introduce benefits, and confiscate the factory owners’ property. Then she laughs: it is a joke, and the newspapers, including The Herald, have been bribed by the dons and portray the workers as idiots eager to wreck railways. The only truth in The Herald is the Esperanza advertisement on the last page. The giant with the iron hand whom Alvaro mistook for Mor in chapters eleven and thirteen is called Little One; he is Mor’s ally.

## Elections, betrayal, and the strike (ch. 22–30)
In chapter twenty-two, Beatriz, proposing theories about the antagonist, names the student Wasp as suspicious. In chapter twenty-six, a mysterious lady, later revealed to be Fatima, explains the political background to Alvaro. The supremo election is six months away: there is a “war party”—generals, Shadow, and some allens of Medicine, Service, and Transport—and a “peace party”—merchants, scholars, metallurgists, and mine owners. On the left stand the Three Bulls, torn apart by the obreros’ revolt; on the right, the “parasites” of Law, Knowledge, and Art. Seven factory owners, including Maduro, have monopolized weapons and steam engines; Mor’s Esperanza has emerged as their competitor, so, the woman says, many esferas would benefit from Mor’s victory. But the Law esfera has suddenly backed the factory owners.

In chapter thirty, Alvaro receives a harsh letter from Mor. She writes that Beatriz is Fatima’s daughter; that Mor did not want to manipulate him and sought an honest bargain; and that Alvaro told Maduro too much, so Maduro is now recruiting “hundreds of Shadows” and preparing for a bloodbath. It turns out that Fernan told Mor of Alvaro’s ignorance: he wanted both a connection to Fatima for the movement and material for the book, and Lucille had warned him. Alvaro strikes his friend with his injured hand but later forgives him.

## By the end of the novel (ch. 34)
In chapter thirty-four, at Last Winter Castle, Alvaro identifies Fernan as Mor of Brega’s assistant and warns Maduro’s mercenary Salvador: if he harms Fernan or Fatima’s daughter Beatriz, Fatima will join forces with Mor against his employer. The novel does not reveal how the strike ends or how it affects the election.
