Catalia is the state in which much of [[sledy-na-vode|Traces on the Water]] takes place and the homeland of two of its three protagonists: the writer [[alvaro-da-silva|Alvaro da Silva]] and the convict [[red|Red]]. It is a country of steam-powered transport, rifled barrels, opera, and judicial debates, where everyone belongs to a professional estate, or esfera, every estate governs itself, and any conflict can become a verbal duel before a jury. Dangarians call Catalians “chatterboxes”; they call themselves “people of the word.”

## Land and cities
The country stretches along the coast. Chapter 9 explains its division: at some point, the former “lower” lands of the south and northeast demanded more respectable names, and people now speak of Beautiful Catalia and Mighty Catalia. Beautiful Catalia is [[laerta|Laerta]] and the entire southern coast, with its warm climate, theaters, and universities. Mighty Catalia is the northeast, with coal mines, factories, and industrial plants. “South of the mountains lives Catalia’s glory; north of them, its strength,” says the narrator. The two railway networks connect at only two gaps in the mountain chain; the largest junction lies in the Ortiyar valley, thirty miles from the capital, guarded by [[ortiyar|Ortiyar Fortress]].

The journey the reader takes in chapter 4 provides a general picture: from Laerta, the train heads northwest through the Darineian Range and the White Bone Pass, crosses rice-growing [[seleziya|Selezia]], then industrial [[brega|Brega]] on the River Monho, then the Kabarda steppes—the land of stubborn men, quarrelsome bulls, and bickering women—and finally reaches the border fortress of [[indra|Indra]], encircled by fortifications. Beyond it lie marshes and [[ollandiya|Ollandia]]. On the author’s map, Indra and [[gerona|Gerona]] are labeled in the northern part of the blue region; farther south are the provinces of Brega and Selezia, Ortiyar Fortress, and the capital, Laerta, on the southern coast.

## Organization
**Esferas.** The basic unit of Catalian society is an esfera, a professional circle, rather than an estate determined by birth. At various points, the novel names the esferas of Art, Law, Craft (also called Production), Service, Agriculture, Shadow, War, Finance, Knowledge, Medicine, and Transport. Laerta has eleven districts, one per esfera, each with its own emblem: a slipper for Service, a cogwheel for Craft, and a bull’s head for Agriculture. In an interview, the author stressed that Catalia is “not a state as we understand it”: each esfera is more like a federation, while society is individualistic and everyone is concerned with personal success. For more about its organization, see [[esfera|Esferas]].

**The High Table.** Shared affairs are decided by the [[vysokij-stol|High Table]], on which each esfera has three [[alleny|allens]], regardless of its population. Once a year, the Table votes out its worst member; the narrator notes that Don Filippe has avoided this fate for six consecutive years. The head of state is the elected supremo. Chapters 22 and 26 say that elections are due in roughly six months, and that the High Table is divided into a “war party”—the military, Shadow, and parts of Medicine, Service, and Transport—and a “peace party”—merchants, scholars, metallurgists, and mine owners. The Three Bulls alliance, comprising Production and related esferas, is called the left wing; Law, Knowledge, and Art form the right.

**Dons and allens.** An esfera’s leader is a don, a title that is generally not hereditary: a don’s status is confirmed or revoked through a debate in the [[palata-slova|Chamber of the Word]]. The exception is the Production esfera, where a don owns a factory; factories pass by inheritance, and a worker, or obrero, cannot buy one (ch. 13). The characters at the Three Bulls frequently debate this imbalance.

**Taxes and customs.** Catalians pay no taxes and keep no register of people: everyone has the right to remain “unrecorded” (ch. 22). Laerta recognizes no import duties (ch. 7, 17), while smuggling handles exports from other countries. The escudo is the main unit of currency; pesetas and pesos also occur in the text.

## Law, the Scroll, and the First Judge (ch. 1–22)
At the heart of Catalian law lies the idea that truth is the outcome of an adversarial debate. Its foundation is the [[pervyj-sudya|First Judge]], who created the system of [[duelnyj-spor|dueling debates]] and the [[svitok|Great Scroll]], where their results are recorded: heroic deeds, personal truths, and awarded bracelets. Entries cannot be forged; they can only be challenged in a new debate. The First Judge’s principles are carved into the floor slabs in the Scroll’s hall: “Skill is the key,” “Common property belongs to no one,” “Honor matters more than morality,” and “One equals seven” (ch. 22).

Any voluntary transaction is lawful; harm is recognized only if the injured party complains; verbal threats are not punishable; and anything can serve as security for a debt (ch. 13, 20). Debates are conducted by an arbiter with a bracelet of the Law esfera and require at least five jurors. Jurors serve without pay and come for the spectacle; the Chamber of the Word is jokingly called a “free theater” (ch. 1). Guards from the Shadow esfera and alguacils from the Law esfera maintain order in the streets.

## White, grey, and black lands
Cities are divided into areas with different levels of safety. On “white land,” in districts such as Art, there are no locks, bars, or guards; murder is so deeply taboo that it is almost impossible for a Catalian, and a “white corpse” results in a brutal execution. On “grey land,” theft, fraud, and fistfights are permitted, but killing and maiming are not. On “black land,” almost anything is allowed; the [[staryj-port|Old Port]] is one example. For more, see [[belaya-zemlya|White, Grey, and Black Land]].

## Society and everyday life
The author defines a Catalian’s social ascent as the “American dream”: rising through one’s own labor from arera to maestro and don. Rank within an esfera is indicated by [[braslet|bracelets]]: an art maestro wears silver, while gold ranks higher. There are bracelets for [[obrero|obrero]] workers, engineers—black with three golden cogwheels—pirates and assassins, and the prim-allen of Law, bearing [[iolanta|Iolanta’s]] white feather; there are also wedding and friendship bracelets. Bracelets are awarded through judicial debate and issued in the Chamber of the Word. Changing esferas is extremely difficult, a constant subject of complaint at the Three Bulls.

Catalians value wit and a positive outlook: they celebrate weddings and divorces alike, though divorce requires a “right of divorce,” which is used up. At funerals, they plant a [[sinkho|sinkho]] sapling on the porch and hang ribbons on it: light-colored or blue ribbons express fond remembrance, while red or black ribbons signal enmity. To “go into the blue” means to die; according to belief, a soul goes after death either into the blue beyond or into the abyss.

## Religion and mythology
Catalia has never had monotheism. Before the First Judge, the country practiced “rather creepy paganism of a Lovecraftian kind”: its gods were the [[drevnie|Ancients]], also called the “lords of the abyss,” sea monsters once worshipped by its people. The opera mentions that the Ancients used to be called the Winged Ones and the Lords of the Abyss. The First Judge abolished this, and today, according to the author, people believe in gods “roughly as we believe in muses”: there are no icons or prayers, and they survive in curses and art. The [[mirny|mirnas]], male and female patrons of every activity from luck ([[eliga|Eliga]]) to mechanics (Darreggio), play an everyday role. For more, see [[alessina|Alessina]], [[iolanta|Iolanta]], [[vinsenya|Vinsenya]], and [[darredzho|Darreggio]]. The author noted that the First Judge has influenced the Catalian worldview more than the ancient gods. In his formulation, Catalia represents “the triumph of materialism”; most of its people see wells as curiosities rather than objects of faith.

## Economy and technology
In Traces on the Water, Catalia is the world’s most advanced country. It builds locomotives, steamships, steam-powered carriages, revolvers, rifled carbines, and howitzers; the [[esperantsa|Esperanza]] factory makes compact steam engines and fast wheeled vehicles sold even in the Muari Emirates. The industrial center is Brega, with its coal and the River Monho. Steam-powered machines displace workers, and chapter after chapter the characters watch unemployment and strikes grow; the Reformist movement and the [[tri-byka|Three Bulls]] alliance oppose the factory owners. Newspapers, including [[vestnik|The Herald]], act as a public forum. For more on technology, see [[tekhnika|Technology]].

## The army and the threat of war (ch. 9, 28, 31)
Catalia’s army is small: by chapter 28, it has fifteen combat-ready divisions—eleven infantry and four cavalry—and the War esfera has shrunk for lack of funds. General [[dezerej|Gabriel Deserey]], stationed at [[ortiyar|Ortiyar]], asks [[fatima|Fatima Ir-Reya]] to raise the issue of mobilization at the High Table: at least two hundred thousand people are needed, at a cost of three hundred million escudos in the first month and one hundred million in every subsequent month. Most of the burden would fall on the Three Bulls, disliked by those at the top. Field Marshal Cortago, Deserey’s commander and one of War’s allens, does not propose mobilization: the Table interprets requests from the military as greed for money.

Dangarian reports (ch. 31) show that Catalia has twenty or thirty rifled artillery pieces, and that two Catalian howitzers with a range of about four versts are operating at [[mershants|Mershants]]. According to a Dangarian general, Catalian carbines shoot twice as far as fusils. Catalia’s railway network permits free maneuver, while Laerta controls the sea.

## Foreign relations (ch. 4, 17, 26, 31)
- **Dangar.** The western empire considers Catalians alien in religion and customs; according to the author, both sides’ rulers fear war, and Dangar would find it difficult to assimilate the Catalians. Throughout the novel, Catalia follows the [[olandskaya-vojna|Ollandian War]] first as news and then as a threat.
- **Ollandia.** The principalities on the way to Dangar have no locomotives of their own—all are Catalian. Catalia trades with them and guards its frontier with [[indra|Indra]].
- **Muaris and Solanta.** According to Count [[lurd|Ludwig von Lurd]] (ch. 17), thirteen years earlier Catalia signed an alliance treaty with the island of [[solanta|Solanta]], formerly the site of Muari bases. Since then, the Muari have lost their supremacy over the Miriam Sea, while trade between Muaris and Laerta has flourished. For more, see [[muaris|Muaris]] and [[solantijskaya-ekspeditsiya|The Solantan Expedition]].
- **Mergana.** The homeland of the philosopher [[liess-van-khalm|Liess van Halm]], on the western edge of the known world as the people of Laerta see it.

## History (ch. 3, 16, 26)
The first book provides little information about Catalia’s past. Known elements include the age of hidalgo knights and paganism before the First Judge—the opera “Señorita Violet” depicts that period; the “Third War of Kings,” during which the count’s castle near Faramo station was destroyed; and the Valentian dynasty that ruled the kingdom of Laertar. Its palace became a museum and restaurants, while an underground passage from the palace leads to a wine cellar. As one episode recounts, the last Valentians fortified the palace, prepared ships for escape, and gave an infanta to a Muari sultan. The [[galereya-eliste|Eliste]] Gallery holds the battle panoramas “The Battle of Lamertina,” “The Conquest of Brega,” and “The Death of King Augustus the Pale.” Recent history is tied to the exploit of the heroes of Solanta, performed sixteen years before the novel begins. The author said that the development of Catalia’s and Dangar’s systems would be explored in later books.

## Language and names
Catalian is rendered in Russian in the novel, but names, titles, clothing, and terminology are based on Spanish: señor and señorita, maestro and don, doña, “ma donna” (used when asking someone of higher rank for a major favor), escudo, alguacils, hidalgo, and “allen.” In an interview, the author explained that Spanish was one of the sources of the idea: he began learning it while inventing this world.

## Conception and interviews
According to the author, Catalia embodies ideas and principles of statehood, as Dangar does. Its outward appearance is modeled on Spain, but “Catalia is not Spain”: the country’s social order is entirely unlike Spain’s. Catalians are materialist pagans whose lives have no place for monotheism; their society is individualistic; and the author compared their dream of rising through work to become a maestro and don with the American dream. The mirnas are not gods but something like muses, and they are “not for worship.”

## By the end of the novel (ch. 28–34)
By the novel’s conclusion, the country faces a choice debated by the characters in the Chamber of the Word and at the High Table: keep trading with Dangar and hope for peace, or prepare for war. Dangarian armies in Ollandia stand at the border; mobilization is under discussion but no decision has been made, and the supremo elections still lie ahead. Meanwhile, the Reformists’ strike continues within the country, and the mysterious “curse” of the house above Tres Molinos is explained by a dark schönbrun and a chain of coincidences involving the heroes of the Solanta expedition.
